Your Camera Maker Has Not Abandoned Photography for Video

November 2, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Camera makers have added video in addition to photography, not instead of it: stills features are mature and hard to improve, video had obvious room to grow, and recent stills-first cameras prove the stills side is still being built.

Why does every release feel video-first?

The complaint is everywhere, and it attaches to every brand in turn: whichever company you shoot with, someone is convinced it has lost interest in photography. DPReview's Richard Butler, writing after a week shooting Canon's EOS R7, calls that reading wrong. Every camera company has become interested in video in addition to photography, and that is not the same thing as instead of it.

The evidence people cite often does not hold up. A fully articulating screen is treated as proof of video priorities, yet stills cameras have carried them for decades: Panasonic's first Micro Four Thirds camera, the G1, had one and shot no video at all.

Where is the stills progress actually happening?

Butler's explanation is simpler than a conspiracy: photographic features are relatively mature. Sensors have been photographically excellent for some time, single-image processing is almost universally good, and ergonomics are the result of decades of iteration. It is genuinely hard to point at a modern camera and name an important photo feature it lacks.

That is also why the stills advances that do arrive cluster in one place: autofocus. On-sensor phase detection and subject recognition are relatively modern systems, and every major company is still refining them. And the recent release lists tell their own story. Alongside video-focused models like Nikon's ZR, the company that bought cinema maker Red also makes the Zf, a deliberate statement of photo heritage.

So should photographers stop worrying?

Mostly, with one honest exception Butler names himself. Adding video features rarely costs stills shooters anything, and expanding the audience to video users subsidizes camera development; a camera without those features risks becoming a niche product sold at a higher price. The genuine complaint is a design trend he ran into on the R7: folding the stills and video switch into the power switch, so reaching for a photo can start a video clip instead.

Why it matters for you

Judge your next camera by the stills it helps you make, not by the feature list it carries. If the autofocus finds the eye, the screen lets you shoot comfortably, and the ergonomics disappear in your hand, the camera serves photography, whatever else it can record. The video features are not taking your camera away. They are, in part, paying for it.
