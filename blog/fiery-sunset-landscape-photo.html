You Don't Need a Fiery Sunset to Make a Great Landscape Photo

October 20, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: No. Landscape photographer Craig Roberts argues most real outings happen under ordinary, pleasant light, and that flat or overcast conditions suit many subjects better than spectacle. Without a fiery sky doing half the work, composition, shape, and texture carry the frame, which builds the creative skills he says are the real challenge.

Why are we so obsessed with epic light?

Most of your time with a camera happens under ordinary skies at ordinary places. Yet the landscape images that get the most attention online are blazing sunsets over famous viewpoints. That gap shapes what you think a successful outing looks like, and makes the scenes you actually encounter feel like second best.

Photographer Craig Roberts takes that assumption apart with a single, unglamorous word: pleasant. His locations are never what you would call epic -- but pleasant is what you see most of the time when you are out with a camera, and that makes it far more relatable than the escapist version of the hobby.

What does flat light do for a photo?

Overcast suits many subjects that hard sun works against. Woodland, waterfalls, wet rock, moss, and autumn leaves often look better under cloud, because the sky acts like an enormous softbox: shadows open up, highlights stay manageable, and colors come through richer.

With no glowing sky to carry the frame, composition, shape, and texture have to do the work. That is why these scenes take more effort than the dramatic ones -- there is no fiery sky doing half the job.

Why shoot locally instead of traveling?

Famous viewpoints now draw heavy crowds; Horseshoe Bend in Arizona has added parking fees and infrastructure to cope. A field, lane, or river bend near home has no such problem, and you can return in fog, frost, rain, and summer haze until you know exactly where the light falls each season.

Between home and epic, as Roberts puts it, sits pleasant: scenes full of potential if you slow down and look. Pick a route you already walk often and stop at anything that catches your eye, even when it feels too ordinary to bother with.

Why it matters for you

Technical skills, Roberts says, are often the easier part. Creative skills -- the ideas and the way of seeing -- are where the real challenge lies. Chasing epic light outsources the seeing to the sky. Pleasant light hands it back to you. The fastest path this year is not a flight to an iconic overlook. It is the same quiet bend in the river, visited twenty times, until you know it better than anyone.
