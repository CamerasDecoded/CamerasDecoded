Getty Is Going Private Through Bankruptcy. Here Is What Photographers Should Do Now

October 13, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Getty Images announced a prepackaged Chapter 11 restructuring that would take the company private, backed by up to $533 million in new equity. Contributor royalty claims are proposed to stay unimpaired, but the plan is not court-approved yet, so photographers should document their balances and preserve their contracts now.

What did Getty actually announce?

Getty announced a restructuring agreement and said it plans to begin a prepackaged Chapter 11 process in the coming weeks. Affiliates of the Getty family and Koch Equity Development have committed up to $533 million in new equity, and if the restructuring completes, those investors would own all of the reorganized, privately held company.

The cash pressure is substantial: debt service, costs tied to the proposed Shutterstock merger, warrant litigation, and Getty's Stability AI litigation. The goal is to preserve the licensing business while shedding debt, which could leave more cash to run it. A possibility for contributors, not a guarantee.

What does it mean for photographers?

The term sheet places general unsecured claims, the category most royalty balances would likely fall into, in Class 6, and proposes leaving allowed claims unimpaired: the plan does not currently aim to withhold contributor payments. That is a proposal, not a ruling.

The term sheet also proposes keeping most contributor contracts in force, though Getty could ask the court to reject particular ones. Do not treat the announcement as permission to ignore exclusivity clauses or revoke licenses. You still own your copyright, Getty's FAQ confirms, but Getty may hold licensing rights under your agreement, so review your contract before acting.

What should you do right now?

Build the paper trail. Download royalty statements and payment history, screenshot your unpaid balance with a date on it, and keep copies outside Getty's systems. Note the exact Getty entity named in your agreement.

Then preserve the contracts themselves: signed agreements, amendments, releases, and an inventory of submitted files, with originals and backups under your control. Read every official notice and calendar its deadlines. Track licensing, reporting, and payment dates separately, because the filing date splits what counts as before versus after. And plan for cash flow: know what a delayed payout would mean, and get a lawyer involved if the amount is substantial.

Why it matters for you

Platform money is platform risk. Royalty income feels passive, but it sits inside someone else's balance sheet. The habit that protects you is the habit this moment demands: keep records you control, read the contract before you act, and never let one platform be the whole business.
