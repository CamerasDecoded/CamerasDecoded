Lightroom's Vectorscope Turns Color Grading From Guesswork Into Measurement

November 16, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Lightroom now has a vectorscope, a circular chart that maps the hue and saturation of every pixel in your photo. It shows whether your white balance is true, whether skin sits on the skin-tone line, and whether your grade follows the palette you intended. Color stops being a feeling and becomes something you can measure.

What is a vectorscope, and what does it actually show?

A vectorscope maps the hue and saturation of every pixel in your photo, the same way a histogram maps exposure. It is a circular chart with the six main colors marked around the edge, and every color in your image shows up as a dot. The closer a dot sits to the edge, the more saturated that color is. A seventh line marks skin tones.

One quirk first: exposure changes what the scope shows. Lightroom displays color through the HSL model, where midtones read as far more saturated than shadows and highlights, so an underexposed photo shows almost everything clustered near the middle.

How does it fix your white balance?

Neutral gray and desaturated colors sit at the center of the circle. To set a proper white balance, aim for your colors to originate from the middle of the scope. When the white balance is off, everything skews and the middle is no longer centered.

Do not force overall balance: a blue sky, green vegetation, and warm sunset light will naturally spread the dots unevenly, and that is correct. And fix the white balance before any color grade, because grading shifts the white point.

What does it do for skin tones?

This is the simplest win in the whole tool. When skin is in the frame, check that it sits along the skin-tone line. Hover over your subject's skin and the scope shows a crosshair for the tones underneath, or mask the face so the scope renders only what is in the mask.

Mask the skin alone, then move White Balance, Tint, and Hue gently. It matters most when the light is doing something strange, like the green cast from portraits shot under foliage. But the scope is a guide, not a rule: a strong stylized grade will pull skin off the line on purpose.

How does it help you hold a consistent grade?

The scope also checks that a grade follows the palette you intended. For an analogous palette, colors next to each other on the wheel, most of your color information should concentrate in one small section of the scope. For a complementary palette, the two dominant colors should sit opposite each other. Watch the distribution in real time and nudge ranges with the Hue and Saturation sliders.

Why it matters for you

Your screen lies to you. Room light, brightness, and tired eyes all shift what you think you see, and color graded by feel drifts from one photo to the next. Open the scope once per edit, check the white balance and the skin line, and your grades will hold together across a whole series.
