Why f/16 Makes Every Photo a Little Softer

October 4, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Every aperture spreads light into a tiny blur called the Airy disc, and it grows in exact proportion to the f-number: about 1.34 microns per f-stop. There is no cliff at f/11. Diffraction is a continuous tax on contrast, paid since f/1.4, which is why an f/16 landscape can look soft at 100 percent and print beautifully on a wall.

What is diffraction, really?

Diffraction is not a lens flaw. Aberrations come from imperfect glass, and better engineering can drive them toward zero. Diffraction is set by the physics of light passing through any opening: shrink the aperture and part of the wavefront is removed, so light spills sideways. Every point of light becomes a tiny blur spot called the Airy disc.

At the middle of the visible spectrum, that disc grows 1.34 microns per f-stop: 5.4 microns at f/4, 10.7 at f/8, 14.8 at f/11, 21.5 at f/16, and 29.5 at f/22. Only the f-number sets it, not the size of the hole. A 24mm lens at f/8 has a 3mm opening and a 600mm at f/8 has a 75mm opening, yet both land a 10.7-micron spot on the sensor. Identically. Format never enters the calculation.

Why is there no cliff at f/11?

Contrast declines smoothly with every stop, from the widest aperture you own. The hard ceiling is the cutoff frequency, the finest detail a perfect lens can transmit at all: 227 line pairs per millimeter at f/8, 165 at f/11, 114 at f/16, 83 at f/22. There is no threshold you cross; it is a tax you have been paying since f/1.4.

That is why one f/16 file tells two stories. At 100 percent on screen it can look measurably soft, yet it still holds the detail a 16 by 20 inch print demands. Same file, same physics: pixel-level scrutiny sees the tax, exhibition-size printing does not.

What do more megapixels change?

Nothing bad. A 61-megapixel file at f/16 holds more real detail than a 24-megapixel file at f/16. Higher resolution does not create the degradation; it lets you see a degradation that was always there. At the same print size, finer pixels never make diffraction worse.

Why it matters for you

Stop fearing f/16 and start choosing apertures on purpose: shoot at the widest aperture that still gives you the depth of field the scene needs. Anything narrower pays the tax for nothing. Note where your lens peaks too, superb designs around f/2.8, modest ones around f/5.6, and past f/11 nearly all lenses converge anyway. When the photograph demands depth of field, take f/16 and do not grieve.
