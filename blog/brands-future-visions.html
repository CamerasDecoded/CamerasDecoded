Canon, Nikon, Sony and Fujifilm Each See Your Future Differently

October 6, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Nikkei asked Canon, Nikon, Sony and Fujifilm where photography is headed, and each answered differently. Canon's Go Tokura is betting on the shooting experience itself and more video-oriented V-series cameras; Nikon's Hiroyuki Ikegami is investing in processors, sensors and video while building a 'smartphone + 1' camera; Sony's Kenji Tanaka bets on Spatial AI digital twins for every kind of creator; Fujifilm's Koji Matsumoto doubles down on the tactile joy smartphones can't deliver.

What does Canon think you want?

Canon executive Go Tokura points at who is actually driving growth: young people buying compact digital cameras. 'They certainly aren't buying them just because of high performance or image quality; they enjoy the shooting experience itself, even if it comes with some minor inconveniences,' he says. Canon's lineup used to be organized around performance and price; now it has to weave in style and feel.

On the roadmap, that means video. Tokura says Canon wants to expand its video-oriented V-series lineup based on customer demand and enrich its video lens selection further — with a specific insistence that lenses made for video must also shoot stills without any issues. Canon's future is cameras you enjoy holding, that do video properly.

What is Nikon's plan?

Nikon executive Hiroyuki Ikegami is honest about where the company stands: 'It is taking a bit of time to cascade the features of our flagship Z9 down to lower-end models,' he says. Completing that high-to-low lineup drops the average selling price, so right now Nikon is concentrating investment on next-generation image processing engines, sensors and video cameras — laying the foundations.

Ikegami also names the device he wants to build: as smartphones get more sophisticated, there's demand for a dedicated camera as a 'smartphone + 1' device that preserves smartphone battery life — products younger demographics will actually pick up. For anyone waiting on affordable Z bodies with Z9-grade tricks, this is the signal that more is coming down the line.

Why does Sony keep saying 'Spatial AI'?

Sony's Kenji Tanaka takes the business in an entirely different direction. 'Spatial AI,' he explains, 'involves capturing the physical world using image sensors to create a digital twin, wherein we can make reality more attractive or simulate different ways of presenting it' — simulation that then becomes content for people to experience back in the physical world.

At first glance that sounds like research-lab talk, but the product meaning is concrete: spatial recognition and subject-tracking technology folded back into Alpha cameras. And Sony no longer thinks of creators as just photographers and filmmakers: 'Anyone who creates something and inspires emotion is a creator,' Tanaka says — dancers, musicians, athletes included. Sony's future is an entertainment company that happens to make cameras.

What is Fujifilm doubling down on?

Fujifilm executive Koji Matsumoto reiterates what the company has said for years: photography needs to be fun. Smartphones democratized photography and gave everyone a more discerning eye, and the demand for cameras among people who care about self-expression has grown. Fujifilm's marketing is built around letting people feel 'the joy of photography' at hands-on shooting events and stores.

The foundation, he argues, is decades of accumulated camera technology: 'Finding affection in the tactile, analog feel of turning dials or enjoying the distinct color tones of film photography is only possible thanks to our accumulated camera technology.' And the counter to AI image generation is blunt: 'AI can generate images, but only humans possess the subjective sense of feeling, This is real.'

Why it matters for you

Read these four answers as buying guidance. If you want the tactile, film-look experience, Fujifilm is betting its whole company on exactly that — it isn't a marketing phase. If video is your growth area, Canon is widening the V-series lane and Nikon's video investment is mid-cascade. And if you're deciding between ecosystems, Sony is telling you plainly that its cameras are becoming tools for every kind of creator, not just photographers. The brands have picked their lanes; the question is which one you're actually standing in.
