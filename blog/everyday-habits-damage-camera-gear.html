Seven Small Habits Are Quietly Wearing Down Your Camera

November 9, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Most camera damage does not come from a dramatic drop. It comes from small, repeated habits: carrying a mounted tripod over your shoulder, swapping lenses in the wind, moving gear from cold air into humidity, and rushing for the light. Photographer Michael Scott breaks down seven of them, and every fix costs seconds, not money.

What actually breaks cameras?

A shattered filter rarely comes from a dramatic fall. Photographer Michael Scott learned the lesson after dropping a filter onto rocks at a small Oklahoma waterfall, and he traces most gear damage back to small habits that stack up over years of shooting. A loose ball head, a lens swap in the wind, a tripod slung over your shoulder: none of them feels dangerous in the moment, and each one quietly raises the odds of a repair bill.

Which habits cost the most?

The expensive ones are the invisible ones. Change a lens on a windy beach and sand can blow into the open mount and settle on your sensor as a spot you will clone out for months. Move gear from a cold house into hot, humid air and condensation forms inside the lens; on a 95-degree morning at 60 percent humidity, a lens from a 70-degree house fogs the instant it meets the air. Scott seals the camera in a bag before the transition and lets it warm up inside the bag, and he has never found moisture inside a lens.

What is the cheapest protection you already own?

Your lens hood. Its stated job is flare control, but it also takes the knocks that would otherwise land on the front element, so Scott mounts it the right way around instead of leaving it reversed. Tripods need the same attention: flip-lock screws back out, twist locks lose lubrication, and one unlocked leg is enough for a gust to bring the whole rig down. Seconds of maintenance beat a new lens. Carry the camera mounted and flip the rig so the lens points down, use a plate with anti-rotation pins, or better still, carry the camera separately.

Why it matters for you

Rushing for the light is the root cause of most of the rest. Scott says most of his own mishaps happened that way, from hoods dropped off cliffs to caps dropped in the water. His fix: arrive early, settle the composition, and decide in advance what you will do if the light turns up somewhere else. Gear care is a skill, like exposure. The camera you look after is the one still working when the light shows up.
