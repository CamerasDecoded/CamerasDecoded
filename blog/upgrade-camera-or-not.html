Is It Time to Upgrade Your Camera? A Marsh Test Answers It

October 6, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Sort your cameras by the job they do. A 2018 body like the Fujifilm X-H1 still makes beautiful files, but subject-detection autofocus — the kind the X-H2S and the Nikon Z8 and Z9 introduced — changed the hit rate on fast-moving subjects more than any megapixel jump. Upgrade the body that shoots the subjects you keep missing; keep the one that's only ever asked to be fun.

What happens when an old camera meets a marsh?

Omar Gonzalez waded into a noisy marsh with a Fujifilm X-H1 — Fujifilm's first body with in-body stabilization, from 2018 — and a Sigma 100-400mm to test a question he has been chewing on. Does struggling with an older camera make you more of a photographer, or does it just lower your odds of getting a great frame?

Wildlife is a tough place to ask it. Birds don't wait while you fight sluggish autofocus and menus that take a few extra presses to reach the setting you want. A field test like this is more useful than a spec sheet, because an older camera's frustrations surface with a bird on a branch and only a few seconds to work — including the moment the X-H1's small battery died mid-shoot and Gonzalez had to dig out one of the three spares he had packed.

What is the one upgrade that actually changes your hit rate?

It is not resolution. Fujifilm's X-H2S, released in 2022, brought subject-detection autofocus that recognizes birds, animals and vehicles, and Nikon's Z8 uses the same detection system as the flagship Z9. That shift has changed the cost of shooting an older body in the field more than any jump in megapixels.

A camera from 2018 can still produce a beautiful file, yet it asks you to place and hold a focus point on a moving bird yourself — which is exactly the task newer bodies now handle on their own. If your shooting involves anything fast, subject detection is the line in the sand between a camera that helps you and one you fight.

How do you decide which cameras to keep?

Gonzalez's own shelf is the case study. His X-T20 is the travel camera because it's tiny; the weather-sealed X-T2 is the rainy-day camera; the X-Pro2, X-E1 and X-E2 wait for a turn that rarely comes. He splits the collection into work cameras and play cameras, and the play cameras get a pass on autofocus because nothing rides on getting the shot.

The practical rule falls out of that: sort each body by the job it actually does. A camera that only comes out for slow, casual walks loses almost nothing to aging autofocus, while the one you count on for fast subjects loses the most. And bodies that sit unused on your shelf may be worth more than you'd guess — when the X100V took off on social media in 2023 and stayed backordered for months, plenty of people started hunting older Fujifilm models as a way into the system, which lifted the used market for exactly the cameras nobody was picking up.

Why it matters for you

The upgrade question stops being emotional the moment you match the camera to the job. If you keep missing the shot because the autofocus can't hold a moving subject, that is a real, nameable deficit and a newer body is the fix. If the camera you want to replace only ever shoots slow, casual frames, its files are as good as they ever were — keep it, shoot it, and spend the money on the thing that will actually change your hit rate.
