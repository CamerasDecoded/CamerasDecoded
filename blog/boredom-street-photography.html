Why Boredom Might Be the Best Thing for Your Street Photography

October 18, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Street photographer Peter Forsgard says street photography is about 95% boredom, and that the dull hours between moments are the ordinary state of the craft, not a failure. He distinguishes deliberate warm-up shots from restless "panic shots," and when restlessness wins, he slows down, finds a coffee, picks one corner, and waits.

Why is street photography 95% boredom?

Most street outings are long stretches where nothing worth photographing happens, and how you handle those stretches shapes what you bring home. Walk for two hours with nothing to show for it and you know the problem.

Forsgard argues the dull part is where your eye gets trained. Reels show the moment an image gets made and skip the hours behind it. An exhibition of 50 prints sits on 10,000 frames that never left a hard drive. A walk with no keepers is not wasted; it is the ordinary state of the craft.

What is the difference between a warm-up shot and a panic shot?

In the first hour, Forsgard rarely makes much, so he takes warm-up shots to get his eye moving. That is deliberate: he heads out with an idea or an ongoing project in mind, like his series on Helsinki's 700 graffiti-painted electrical boxes, something for when the street goes quiet.

Later, if boredom sets in and he starts shooting just to have something on the card, those are panic shots, where most bad images come from. The signs are physical: you walk faster, cover more ground, drift without direction. Restlessness is the enemy. His advice: stay bored and let it sharpen your attention.

How do you stay sharp when nothing is happening?

When restlessness wins anyway, his fix is low-tech: stop for a coffee and watch people in the cafe until you feel calmer. Back on the street, pick one corner and stay with it rather than roaming. That usually pulls him out of the rut.

Flat light works the same way: it forces you to watch what people are doing instead of leaning on the light. Contact sheets showed film photographers the misses beside the frame that got printed. Digital removed the cost of each frame, so the discipline moved from the darkroom into your own head.

Why it matters for you

The next time an outing produces nothing, resist the urge to cover more ground. Pick one corner and give it an hour. Head out with a small ongoing project so the quiet stretches have a purpose. The frames you do take will carry more intent, and the slow 95% will feel like training, not failure.
