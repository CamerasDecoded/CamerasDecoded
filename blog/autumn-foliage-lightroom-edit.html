How to Edit Autumn Foliage Without Overcooking It

October 20, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Skip the global saturation and vibrance sliders and edit each hue deliberately. Editor Pit Haupert works fall foliage in Lightroom's color mixer and RGB curves instead — nudging yellow toward orange, pulling orange down, and muting off-season colors — so warm tones stay rich without clipping into a flat neon blob.

Why do fall edits go wrong so easily?

Most fall edits in Lightroom go the same way: you open a frame full of yellow leaves, push vibrance and saturation, and end up with something worse than what came out of the camera. Warm hues clip and smear fast, and a heavy hand turns a glowing tree into a flat neon blob.

Video editor Pit Haupert's fix starts with restraint. He leaves the global saturation and vibrance sliders alone entirely as he works a sunset photo of a ginkgo tree in Vienna: a slight lift in the shadows and whites, a bump in texture, and clarity around minus 20 so the image blooms without going dreamy. In the tone curve, a lifted black point and a gentle S-curve finish the base.

How should you handle the colors?

The real work happens in the color mixer, and it starts with a step most people skip: check which colors are actually in the frame, then adjust each one deliberately. Orange dominates this scene, so its saturation drops to about minus 15. Yellow is the hero and gets only a small saturation lift, with its hue nudged toward orange.

Stripping out off-season colors builds what painters call an analogous palette: neighboring hues on the wheel, here red, orange, and yellow, carry the image, and everything outside that range gets muted.

What about the glow and the light?

Cool shadows with warm highlights -- the old split-toning look, now in the Color Grading panel -- comes from the RGB curves. Haupert treats them gently: three points on each channel, small adjustments, a subtle S-curve into red, a hint of green in the highlights, blue in the shadows.

A blown-out left side gets fixed with an inverted radial gradient over the sun -- a custom vignette he can move anywhere. Inside it: darkened corners, pulled highlights, lowered saturation. Then negative dehaze adds mist and mood instead of more exposure.

Why it matters for you

Fall color is one of the most photographed subjects on earth and one of the most overprocessed. The lesson is not a recipe but a discipline: never touch a global slider when a local one exists, and never push a color before asking what the frame already has. That habit travels to every genre.
