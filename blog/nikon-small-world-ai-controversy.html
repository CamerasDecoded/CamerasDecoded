A Microscope Video Won Nikon’s Big Prize. Scientists Say AI Made It.

October 1, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Nikon is re-reviewing its Small World in Motion winner after researchers found continuity errors and a SynthID watermark in Dr. Ning Xu's cilia video — the marker of AI generation. The authenticity debate has reached the laboratory, and it is coming for your portfolio next: keep your RAWs and know what your edits did.

What happened to the winning video?

The winner of Nikon’s Small World in Motion competition — a celebration of microscopy’s beauty — is under investigation. Scientists spotted continuity errors in Dr. Ning Xu’s winning cilia video: structures popping in and out of existence, sizes that do not match known biology. One researcher ran the footage through Google’s detection and found a SynthID watermark, the invisible marker of AI generation.

Dr. Xu acknowledged using an AI model in post-processing — to distinguish and color structures, he said — while denying he broke the competition’s rule that AI-generated videos are not permitted. His LinkedIn responses have since disappeared, along with his profile. Nikon says it is carefully re-reviewing the win. Scientists say the whole thing could be settled if Dr. Xu shares his raw grayscale footage.

Why it matters for you

The line between processing and generating is now the most contested line in photography, and it is moving toward you. Contests already ask. Clients are starting to ask. Newsrooms like Reuters already run capture-authenticity workflows for exactly this reason.

Build the habit now: keep your RAWs, know exactly what your edits did, and disclose what matters. Trust is becoming a feature of the work itself — the photographers who can prove what is real will be the ones people believe.
