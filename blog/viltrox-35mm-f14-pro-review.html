A Sharp 35mm f/1.4 for $829. The Catch Weighs 760 Grams.

November 1, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: PetaPixel's review of the $829 Viltrox 35mm f/1.4 Pro finds a lens that is sharp across the frame even wide open, with one real cost: 760 grams of it. For Sony shooters the Sigma Art remains the neater buy, but for Nikon Z owners, where Sigma does not play, this is the fast 35mm to take seriously.

What does sharp at f/1.4 actually buy you?

Most fast primes ask you to stop down before the corners clean up. This one does not, according to the review: the center is full of usable detail at f/1.4, the corners hold clarity right to the edge with very little vignetting, and stopping down only tightens things further. That matters because a 35mm f/1.4 is bought for low light and separation, and a lens that only delivers its best stopped down defeats its own purpose. Flare resistance is good, with some minor green ghosting wide open, and the bokeh is pleasing, with the cat's eye effect at f/1.4, a touch of halo around highlights, and polygonal highlights when stopped down.

Where does it fall short?

The weakness the review names is longitudinal chromatic aberration: green and magenta fringing at wide apertures that is difficult to remove in editing and can distract in real pictures. It is not out of control, but it is visible, and it is the kind of flaw that shows up in high-contrast edges rather than test charts. The other cost is physical. At 26.8 ounces, with a 77mm filter thread, this is a big chunk of glass, and the handling reflects that: a beautifully tuned manual focus ring, a proper aperture ring with third-stop clicks and a de-click switch, a customizable button, and a voice-coil motor to drive the heavy elements. Everything feels solid, and everything weighs something.

Does the mount change the verdict?

Yes, and this is the part of the review worth reading twice. On Sony E-mount, the competition is fierce: the Sony G-Master 35mm f/1.4 is better but wildly expensive, and the Sigma 35mm f/1.4 Art is near-perfect, smaller, and lighter, so the Viltrox saves you some money without clearly winning. On Nikon Z, the picture flips. Sigma lenses are not supported on the mount, and the affordable Nikkor 35mm f/1.4 sits a tier below, so the Viltrox becomes the serious fast 35mm at a similar price. The review recommends it outright on Nikon, and with a weight warning everywhere else.

Why it matters for you

Judge a fast prime by what it does at its widest aperture, not by the aperture printed on the barrel, and judge it in your hand, not on a spec sheet. If you shoot Nikon Z and want a fast 35mm, this is a genuine option at $829. If you shoot Sony, try the Sigma first and ask whether the savings are worth carrying an extra half-pound all day. Either way, check high-contrast edges for fringing before you fall in love with a sample gallery.
