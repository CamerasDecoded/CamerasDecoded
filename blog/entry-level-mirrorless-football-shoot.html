Top-Flight Football on an Entry-Level Camera

October 3, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photography student Chantelle Cook shot a Scottish Premiership match with an entry-level Canon EOS R50, shadowing Motherwell FC club photographer Raymond Davies for Digital Camera magazine's Apprentice feature. Her takeaway: in sports photography, the autofocus settings — subject detection, sensitivity, and AF Stop — matter far more than the camera body in your hands.

Who shot the match, and with what?

Chantelle Cook is a photography student at Edinburgh College who only picked up a digital camera for the first time in 2023, and her comfort zone is commercial work, fashion and portraiture in particular. For Digital Camera magazine's Apprentice feature, she stepped far outside it, shadowing Raymond Davies, the club photographer of Motherwell FC, at a Scottish Premiership clash with Falkirk on April 4, 2026. Raymond shoots a pro-grade Canon EOS R5 Mark II. Chantelle shot the match on her own entry-level Canon EOS R50, a 24.2-megapixel APS-C camera. The article never names the lens she used.

What are the three settings that actually mattered?

Chantelle arrived hoping to learn the exact shutter speeds to choose, and here is the honest twist: the article never prints them. What it does print is a technique assessment with three autofocus lessons that matter more than any recipe. First, set subject detection for the right subject, for football that means people or athletes, depending on your camera. Second, use Servo or Continuous AF so you never refocus for each shot, and turn the sensitivity down so players and objects passing between you and your subject do not yank focus off. Third, use the AF Stop buttons on the lens to pause autofocus when the subject will stay at the same focus distance, which keeps focus from racking to the background and raises your hit rate.

Why it matters for you

The day's real lesson is that pro sports images come from autofocus discipline, not the camera tier. A student who started shooting in 2023, working with an R50 against her mentor's R5 Mark II, came home with images the magazine calls worthy of a pro. The gap was approach and three settings, not megapixels. It also pays to know the game, since the techniques differ from one sport to another. Before your next fast-moving subject, learn where your camera's subject-detection modes and AF sensitivity control live, because you will not be reading the manual pitchside. As Chantelle put it from in front of the action, sports photography is the exact opposite of studio work, where everything is planned.
