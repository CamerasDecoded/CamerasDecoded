Every Flash Photo Is Really Two Exposures

October 16, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Flash photographer JC Carey treats every lit photograph as two separate exposures: one set by the camera for the ambient light, one set by the strobe for the subject. His process never changes order: ISO, focal length, aperture, shutter speed, then the light. Aperture comes first, chosen for depth of field and never for exposure.

Why do memorized flash settings keep falling apart?

Most photographers who freeze up with flash have memorized a power number that worked once. Then the sun moves or the room gets darker and the number fails, because the number was never the point. Photographer JC Carey calls his approach a process instead of a recipe. The order never changes: ISO, focal length, aperture, shutter speed, then the light.

What does the order look like in practice?

In the barn, Carey starts at f/4 on a 35mm lens and sets 1/100 s to keep the doorway looking reasonable while the model deliberately falls into shadow. When the subject comes out too dark with the flash on, he leaves every camera setting alone and turns the strobe up about two-thirds of a stop. Reaching for the aperture dial would undo his depth-of-field decision.

The flash-off comparisons make the case. Without the strobe, he drops to 1/60 s and still has to push ISO to 1,000 before the model looks right. By then the barn is fine and the doorway is completely blown, because ISO brightens everything at once. With the strobe back on, he can drag the shutter for ambient mood while the burst of flash freezes the subject.

How does this map to a mirrorless camera?

Most mirrorless bodies simulate exposure in the electronic viewfinder, so when you underexpose the ambient on purpose, the preview looks dark. The flash is not in it yet. Sony, Canon, and Nikon all include a setting to turn the simulation off: Sony calls it Live View Display or Setting Effect, Canon uses Exposure Simulation, and Nikon labels it Apply Settings to Live View. Leave simulation on while you set aperture and shutter, since that preview is an honest picture of your ambient exposure. Switch it off once the strobe is doing the work. The screen becomes a tool for the first exposure, and a test shot handles the second.

Why it matters for you

Flash stops being guesswork when each control owns one job: shutter speed steers the ambient, aperture steers sharpness, the strobe steers the subject. Next time you face a dark room or harsh noon sun, run the order instead of reaching for a remembered number.
