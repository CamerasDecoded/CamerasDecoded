The Best Bird Photo Starts With Standing Still for an Hour

November 13, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: RicohMatt, a retired Adobe employee photographing birds in West Virginia, arrives before sunrise and stands motionless up to an hour, waiting for songbirds to return to the same perch. His kit is a Nikon Z8 with a 180-600mm lens, manual mode with auto ISO, and a monopod. But the photographs come from patience, observation, and natural light, not the gear.

What does his morning actually look like?

He arrives at a meadow, riverbank, pond, or marsh just before sunrise and stays for three to four hours, much of it standing motionless in one spot while birds are active. Songbirds often return to the same perch, and sometimes sit still for long stretches.

The places are ordinary, close to home: the Shenandoah Valley and the West Virginia Panhandle, within fifteen minutes of his door. The rivers bring eagles and herons; the meadows bring cardinals and bluebirds. Some mornings he goes home with nothing but a tick crawling up his leg, and he still calls it a good morning.

What is he shooting with, and what does it do for him?

His camera is a Nikon Z8, with a NIKKOR Z 180-600mm for the birds and a 24-120mm f/4 for everything else. The kit he talks about most is not a lens at all: a carbon-fiber monopod that lets him hold the rig steady far longer than he could by hand while he waits for a bird to take flight.

He shoots almost always in manual mode with auto ISO, leaning on exposure compensation. One minute the subject is a heron in a shaded pool, the next an egret in full morning sun. Manual with auto ISO lets him hold the shutter speed and aperture he wants while the camera absorbs the change in light.

What should a learner copy first?

Copy the approach before the kit. His stated method is patience, observation, and natural light, and the aim is not to document every bird he meets but to show its beauty and its connection to the place it lives in. He believes that purpose is what elevates the photograph.

The practical version is simple: let the birds get used to your presence, guess your settings before the bird arrives, and accept that you will have only seconds to react. Standing still is a skill, and it is the one this photographer has practiced longest.

Why it matters for you

You do not need a Z8 or a 600mm lens to use this. The transferable part is arriving early, picking one perch, and waiting. Try it once: one morning, one spot, no chasing, and see what comes to you.
