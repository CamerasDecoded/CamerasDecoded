Six AI Retouching Myths, Tested on Real Portraits

November 18, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: The test found that modern AI retouching can preserve pores and freckles, hold deep skin tones, and run offline with no per-photo fees. But it was sponsored by the software being tested, so treat the verdicts as a starting point, not proof. Judge any retouching tool yourself: zoom to 100 percent and check texture, tone, and where the files go.

What was actually tested?

Portrait photographer Joe Fraga, based in Salvador, Brazil, took six of the most common objections to AI skin retouching and tested each one against real client work, including a 24-portrait corporate headshot session. The tool under test was Reblum, a desktop retouching app, and the article discloses that it sponsored the piece.

That sponsorship does not make the findings useless, but it sets the terms. A sponsored test can show you what a tool does well. It cannot tell you what to buy. The useful part is the checklist Fraga used, because it works on any retoucher.

Which myths failed?

Plastic skin failed first. Reblum is built on frequency separation, the technique working retouchers use to even out tone without touching texture, and at 100 percent the pores, freckles, and fine lines around the eyes were still there. The darker-skin objection failed too: on the deepest skin in the set, hotspots came down without turning gray, and the warm brown tone did not shift.

The cloud objection failed as well. The software runs on the photographer's own computer, works with no internet connection after a one-time model download, and charges no per-photo fees, so a 24-portrait session exported in under five minutes with Wi-Fi off. Group photos mostly passed: it found all three faces in a team portrait and retouched each one separately, though shine removal could not yet be set per person.

What should you take from a sponsored test?

The durable lesson is the method, not the brand. Fraga's check was the same every time: zoom to 100 percent, look at texture, look at tone on the darkest skin in the frame, and ask where the files live. Any tool that passes those three checks is worth a trial on your own photos.

The honest limit is worth noting too. The tool handles about 80 percent of the repetitive skin work, by the maker's own account, and the rest, stray hairs, deep creases, clothing, backgrounds, still needs a person. A retoucher that claims to replace the retoucher is selling you something else.

Why it matters for you

Retouching is where client trust lives. A tool that flattens skin or shifts tone costs you the client, not just the edit, and no demo reel will show you that failure. Evaluate the tool yourself with the three checks above, on your own hardest files, before it ever touches a paying job. Your judgment is the product. The software is just the shortcut.
