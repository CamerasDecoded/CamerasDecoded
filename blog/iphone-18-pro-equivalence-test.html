Your Phone Is Not Outplaying Physics

November 23, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: When DPReview shot the iPhone 18 Pro and the OM System OM-1 with the same total light reaching each sensor, the images came out nearly identical once both got the same noise reduction. The iPhone's sensor is not in a different league. Its edge is computational: frame stacking and heavy noise reduction, applied whether you want it or not.

What did DPReview actually test?

The iPhone 18 Pro's main camera against the Micro Four Thirds OM-1, both at a 24mm equivalent focal length and 1/60 of a second. The OM-1's sensor has about 3.15 times the surface area of the iPhone's main sensor, a 1.65-stop advantage in total light. So the iPhone shot at F1.8 and ISO 1250 while the OM-1 shot at F3.2 and ISO 4000, capturing the same total amount of light.

There is one catch. The iPhone's new Pro controls let you set shutter speed manually, but doing so switches off the frame stacking that makes iPhone photos so clean in low light. With stacking out of the picture, this was a fair fight between the sensors themselves.

So why does the iPhone look so clean?

Straight out of the camera, the iPhone image looked cleaner than the OM-1's, but it held slightly less fine detail, the signature of noise reduction. Even with frame stacking bypassed, Apple applies heavy, sophisticated noise reduction to every image, including ProRAW files. There is no way to turn it off.

To find out whether the difference came from the sensor or the processing, DPReview ran the OM-1's raw file through Adobe's AI Denoise until it matched the iPhone image. Viewed at the same size, the two images were very similar in noise and detail. If the iPhone's sensor were dramatically more efficient, it would hold noticeably more detail at the same noise level. It does not.

What does a bigger sensor actually buy you?

Control. With an F1.8 lens, the OM-1 would capture about 1.65 stops more total light than the iPhone did in this test. More importantly, you decide how much noise reduction to apply, and when. The phone decides for you, silently, on every frame.

Equivalence is a framework for comparison, not a rule for how to shoot. Nobody has to use equivalent settings. The point is that sensor size is still physics, and no amount of clever software changes how much light a sensor can gather.

Why it matters for you

Total light is the currency of image quality, and once you understand it, every camera decision gets simpler. A phone's magic is software you cannot steer. A real camera's advantage is the decisions it hands back to you: how much light to gather, how much noise to accept, how much detail to keep.

One practical warning from the test: think twice before using the iPhone's manual shutter speed. It switches off the frame stacking that produces the clean low-light images. The control costs you the very thing that makes the phone good. Learn the trade-off before you reach for the dial.
