What f/1.2 Actually Buys You After Dark

October 3, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Jack Wang of Bokeh Therapy spent a night on city streets with Nikon's Z 35mm, 50mm, and 85mm f/1.2 S primes, weighing them against the slower glass he already owns. The f/1.2 lenses render street lamps beautifully, but with modern sensors the real payoff is rendering, how the lens separates layers, not the extra stop of light.

How do the f/1.2 lenses actually compare?

Nikon now has three autofocus f/1.2 primes for Z mount, a 35mm, a 50mm, and an 85mm, each weighing more than a kilogram. Jack Wang of Bokeh Therapy spent a night on city streets with all three, weighing each against the slower glass he already owns. He has a day job and shoots almost entirely after dark, so for him f/1.2 means faster shutter speeds and lower ISO under street lamps.

The 35mm was the surprise. Shooting a tiny toy camera from 30 centimeters with crops at 400 percent, he found depth of field at f/1.2 so thin that details 2 to 3 millimeters apart land on opposite sides of sharp. The f/1.2 is also sharper wide open than the f/1.8 S at f/1.8, and its ARNEO coat diffuses street lamps with a soft glow, without ghosting or lost contrast.

The 50mm's OLED info panel lit up unexpectedly at night, more distracting than helpful. The 85mm breaks the pattern: Nikon makes no 85mm f/1.4 for Z, and the 85mm f/1.8 S is sharper wide open than the f/1.2. The 85mm is about 100 grams heavier than the 35mm, yet its thicker barrel spreads the weight so evenly it handled best of the three.

If sensors handle ISO, what is f/1.2 actually for?

Modern sensors handle high ISO so well that an extra stop rarely makes or breaks a night exposure. That pushes the case for f/1.2 toward rendering: how a lens separates layers, how it draws a neon sign, how much blur you get from a comfortable distance. Depth of field depends on subject distance as much as aperture. A faster lens lets you step back, keep perspective natural, and still melt the background, where at arm's length even f/1.8 blurs plenty.

Why it matters for you

Before lusting after fast glass, test lenses at your shooting distance, not at the counter. The finding that the 85mm f/1.8 S out-sharpens the f/1.2 wide open would save you a fortune if the slower lens suits how you shoot. And note who tested: a photographer with a day job who shoots almost entirely after dark. Evaluate every gear claim against the conditions you actually shoot in.
