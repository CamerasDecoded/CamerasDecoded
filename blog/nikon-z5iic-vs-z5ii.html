Nikon's Z5IIC Drops the Viewfinder — Should You?

October 5, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: The Nikon Z5IIC is a Z5II without its electronic viewfinder: same stabilized sensor, autofocus, and video, minus the EVF, one card slot, the joystick, and one custom mode-dial position. At launch prices, DPReview says buy the Z5II — but the C is built to get cheap.

What did Nikon actually cut?

The headline cut is the electronic viewfinder. The Z5IIC has no EVF at all, so you compose everything on the rear screen — a 3.2-inch articulating LCD at 1.04 million dots, versus 2.1 million on the Z5II. You will miss the viewfinder most on bright sunny days.

Almost everything else survives. Both cameras share the same stabilized sensor, mechanical shutter, burst rates, ports, and video modes, plus modern subject-detecting autofocus. DPReview calls it a lesson in being inexpensive without feeling cheap — at launch it is the joint second least expensive full-frame camera ever made.

What else changes with the cheaper body?

Storage drops from two card slots to one, which matters for once-in-a-lifetime events and is a non-issue for most learners. The joystick is gone, so the focus point moves via touchscreen or pad. The mode dial carries two custom settings instead of three — and in the freed slot sits a new Scene setting, a first for Nikon's full-frame line, that auto-optimizes for sports or dusk scenes. Friendly for a first camera, though learning those decisions yourself remains the better move.

Is the saving actually worth it?

Here is the catch: at launch, a standard Z5II can be had for about the same money, sometimes less. DPReview's advice is straightforward — if that is still true when you read this, buy the Z5II.

But the Z5IIC was clearly designed to get cheap, and MSRP is an opening bid. Once street prices drop below the Z5II, the math flips: same sensor, same images, fewer features you may not miss yet. If every dollar goes toward a lens, that is the moment to pounce.

Why it matters for you

The EVF decision is really a workflow decision — composing at arm's length, fighting glare, draining the battery faster. But everything you are learning on full frame, from depth of field to high-ISO behavior, works exactly the same without one.

Treat this as the broader buying lesson: do not pay for features as insurance. Ask what you will actually use in your first year. If the honest answer is a big sensor and a forgiving body, the cheapest full-frame door that opens for you is the right one.
