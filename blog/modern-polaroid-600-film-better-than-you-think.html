Polaroid 600 Film Is Better Than You Think, and It Teaches Exposure

November 21, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Polaroid discontinued its 600 film in 2008, but The Impossible Project rebuilt it from scratch, and the current generation is far better than the early revivals that earned the film its bad reputation. At ISO 640 with a 5 to 15 minute develop, it is also one of the fastest exposure teachers there is: one frame, one cost, no second chance.

How did a dead film come back?

Polaroid announced it was discontinuing Type 600 film in 2008, and that looked like the end of it. Instead, three people, Florian Kaps, Marwan Saba, and Andre Bosman, formed The Impossible Project, leased an old Polaroid plant building in the Netherlands, bought the old equipment, and hired former Polaroid engineers to build a replacement film from scratch.

The company later acquired the Polaroid name in 2017. The early Impossible films were rough: they faded quickly, washed out if you did not shield them as they ejected, and varied wildly from shot to shot. That reputation stuck for years, long after the film itself had moved on.

What is the film actually like now?

The current generation is a different product. The black and white 600 film has crisp, deep shadows, fairly neutral tones with a touch of ivory warmth, and a visible but handsome grain at ISO 640, developing in 5 to 10 minutes depending on air temperature.

The color 600 film is richer, with higher contrast, deeper blocked-up shadows, and highlights that run warm and creamy rather than stark white. It can still throw in an unpredictable color cast, but that is closer to charm than failure now, and it takes 10 to 15 minutes to develop. There is also i-Type, the same chemistry without the battery in the pack, for Polaroid's modern rechargeable cameras.

What should you know before shooting it?

Remember the speed: 600 film is ISO 640, against 160 for SX-70, which makes it the better choice in dimmer light, and most 600 cameras have a built-in flash. Shield the film the moment it ejects, tuck it away from light for the full developing time, or it will fade and wash out.

And keep it away from airport X-rays: never put 600 film in checked luggage, ask for a hand check at security instead. In vintage bodies the film pack carries the battery, so a fresh pack means a fresh battery every time you load.

Why it matters for you

Instant film is the fastest exposure teacher there is. Every frame costs real money, there is no reprint and no second take, and the result develops in your hand. That forces the two habits every beginner needs: slow down, and actually look before you press the shutter.

You do not need to shoot film to take the lesson: treat your next ten frames like they cost something, and check the exposure before you shoot, not after. The photographers who learn that on a 600 camera never forget it on a mirrorless one.
