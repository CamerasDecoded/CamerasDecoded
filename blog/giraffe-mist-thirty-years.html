Thirty Years of Looking for One Giraffe in the Mist

November 27, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Some photographs cannot be scheduled, only prepared for. Paul Goldstein spent 30 years looking for a giraffe in mist in Kenya before the conditions, the animal, and his position finally aligned for about five minutes. The resulting image won the Mammals category at Nature Photographer of the Year.

What did thirty years of failing actually look like?

The photograph you want already exists in your head. The work is being there, ready, on the one morning it happens in front of you. Wildlife photographer Paul Goldstein describes his winning frame plainly: for 30 years, whenever there was mist, he looked for giraffes, and failed. The problem, as he puts it, is that once you are in the mist, it is virtually impossible to find anything. Digital Camera World reported the story on November 27. The image, made in Kenya's Kicheche Laikipia conservation area on the northern plains of Laikipia in front of Mt Kenya, won the Mammals category at Nature Photographer of the Year.

What finally made the frame work?

Preparation meeting a five-minute window. The mist came from the previous night's rain, the altitude, and the cold, clinging to the plains only briefly before sunrise. Goldstein worked with guide William Wanyika, who favors very early starts, and positioned further back with a 600mm lens, compressing the perspective so the giraffe sits inside the soft mist rather than in front of it. Five minutes after the shutter clicked, both the giraffe and the mist were gone. Judge Steffen Foerster praised the result for its quiet, ethereal quality and simplicity: a giraffe peeking out of pale mist under a pastel sky. Nothing in the frame is dramatic. Everything in it was earned by showing up early for three decades.

Why it matters for you

You do not need 30 years or a Kenyan plain to use this. You need one picture you keep returning for: a street in fog, a field at first light, a subject in weather that only happens a few mornings a year. Write it down, learn what conditions create it, and go back when those conditions appear instead of shooting whatever is convenient. Most frames are made in minutes. The photographers who get them are the ones who were already there.
