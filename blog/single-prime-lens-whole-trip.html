What Happens When You Take One Lens on a Trip

October 2, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer Max Kent packed three lenses for a trip and ended up shooting everything on his Nikon 35mm f/1.8. The single-lens constraint builds visual consistency and eliminates in-the-moment gear decisions — making it one of the cheapest ways to train your eye.

What did the one-lens experiment reveal?

Photographer and YouTuber Max Kent packed for his first big trip abroad the way most of us do: he brought everything — the 35mm, the 50mm, the zoom. Then he noticed something. Across the whole trip, he kept reaching for the same lens — his Nikon 35mm f/1.8 — while the rest rode along untouched.

The first benefit he describes is consistency of look. Open a photo book by a photographer you admire and you will often find the same focal length repeating page after page. A focal length does not create a style by itself, but it feeds into one, and viewers sense the unity even when they cannot say why a set of images feels coherent.

Why do fixed-lens cameras keep selling out?

Camera makers have been quietly agreeing with him for years. The Fujifilm X100VI, the Ricoh GR III, and the Leica Q3 all have fixed lenses, so the focal length decision is made once, at the moment of purchase. The appeal is simplicity: nothing to choose between.

The second benefit is fewer decisions in the moment. Kent compares a full bag to a parking lot with dozens of open spaces — you circle because you cannot settle. Every second spent weighing which lens suits a scene is a second you are not watching the light, the timing, or the person in front of you.

Why it matters for you

The single-lens month is the cheapest photography course you will ever take. Pick a prime you already own and shoot nothing else for thirty days. The first week feels restrictive. After that, you start framing scenes at that angle of view before the camera reaches your eye. You are not learning a lens. You are learning to see.
