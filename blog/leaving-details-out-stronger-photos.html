The Strongest Photos Leave Something Out

November 19, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: A photograph that explains everything is easy to forget. Photographer Max Kent argues that curiosity holds attention: leave a detail out, a face, a cause, what lies past the frame, and the viewer starts imagining the story instead of just recognizing the subject.

What does leaving something out actually look like?

Kent makes his point with a box. He keeps its contents hidden through most of his video, encouraging viewers to wonder what might be inside. When he finally opens it, the box is empty. The reveal is deliberately anticlimactic, but it proves the point: the anticipation was more interesting than the answer.

He points to photographer Sam Stone, whose images invite viewers to imagine what happened before or after the moment captured. A photograph might show footprints without revealing who made them, a shadow without showing the person casting it, or a hand pressed against a window. These details introduce a story without spelling it out.

Why does mystery hold attention?

A beautiful photo can make someone stop scrolling, but an intriguing one makes them look twice and wonder what is happening outside the frame. That sense of mystery gives viewers room to interpret an image. Instead of simply recognizing a subject, they start imagining the circumstances around it. Kent compares this to a magic trick: knowing exactly how it works can take away some of its appeal.

Does this mean confusing photos are good?

No. Kent's point is not to make photographs confusing for the sake of it. The missing detail has to invite a story, not hide one. A footprint suggests someone walked here. A shadow suggests someone is standing just out of view. The difference is intent: a confusing photo leaves the viewer with nothing to hold, while a curious photo leaves one clear question and enough clues to start answering it.

How do you practice it?

Kent's exercise is simple. Look through photography books and pay attention to the images that make you pause. Ask what questions they raise and which details encourage you to imagine a story. Then take that eye out with you, and look for smaller details and moments that suggest a story instead of the obvious attractions.

Why it matters for you

Every night in the Arena, your photo is judged blind by people who have never met you and owe you nothing. The images that win are rarely the ones that explain themselves fastest. They are the ones that make a voter stop, look twice, and wonder. That pause is the whole game. So the next time you lift your camera, consider what you can leave outside the frame.
