Two Ways to Beat Bright Sun With a Flash

November 5, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Most cameras sync with a flash only up to about 1/250 of a second, so a portrait at f/1.2 in bright sun is a problem: faster speeds leave dark bands. The fixes are high-speed sync, which pulses the flash like continuous light at the cost of power, and a global shutter, which exposes every pixel at once.

Why does flash hit a wall in bright sun?

A normal flash fires one quick burst, which works when the shutter is fully open. Push the shutter speed past the sync limit and the sensor is never fully exposed at once: a narrow slit travels across it instead, so a single burst can only light part of the frame. That is the wall you hit when you want a wide aperture in bright light. You want f/1.2 for a soft background, but the sun forces a fast shutter, and the fast shutter breaks the flash.

What does high-speed sync actually do?

It turns the flash into a rapid string of lower-intensity pulses that behave almost like continuous light, so every part of the frame gets lit for as long as the slit needs to cross the sensor. The price is power. Because the light is spread over a longer window, each part of the sensor sees less of it, and the faster you set the shutter, the less light lands on any given spot. In the test, the flash sat at power 5.2 in high-speed sync.

What changes with a global shutter?

On the Sony a9 III, every pixel is exposed at the same instant, so the flash can go back to firing a single strong burst. With compatible gear it syncs up to 1/80,000 of a second, and 1/16,000 at f/1.2. The power difference is striking: Quiles dropped the same flash to 0.1, its lowest setting, and still got a full exposure on his subject. At nearly $6,800 with a 24-megapixel sensor, the a9 III is a specialist tool, not a learner's purchase.

Why it matters for you

The lesson is not the camera, it is the separation of two controls. Shutter speed sets how bright the background looks, and the flash sets how bright your subject looks. A global or leaf shutter lets you push those two apart much further than a standard body can. On the camera you already own, an ND filter does the same job: it cuts ambient light so you can stay at native sync speed and keep the flash's full burst. Once you know which control does what, bright sun becomes a look you can choose.
