Your Vintage Lens Is Not Broken. It Is Just Honest About Light

November 25, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Vintage lenses flare on mirrorless bodies because uncoated or lightly coated glass reflects about 4 percent of light at every air-to-glass surface, and those reflections bounce inside the lens until some reach the sensor as haze or ghosting. Modern coatings cancel those reflections, which is why new lenses hold contrast in backlight that older glass cannot.

Why does old glass wash out in backlight?

Start with the window test. At night you can see the street outside and your own reflection in the glass at the same time, because uncoated glass reflects roughly 4 percent of the light hitting it straight on. Every element has two surfaces, and a modern zoom can hold more than a dozen elements. Add aperture blades, barrel walls, and dust, and stray light bounces around until some lands on the sensor. Photographer David Bergman, answering a viewer question in his Ask David Bergman series covered by Fstoppers, separates the damage into two kinds. Veiling flare is the overall haze that lifts your blacks and flattens color. Ghosting is the distinct rings and streaks that appear when a bright source sits in or near the frame.

Digital bodies make it worse in a way film never did. Film reflects little light, but a sensor assembly behaves more like a mirror, so light can bounce off the sensor, strike the rear element, and return, a path older lenses were never designed around. Cheap adapters with shiny interiors add reflections, and front filters add two more reflective surfaces. Half a century of haze or fungus scatters light further, and worn coatings cannot be repaired at home.

What do coatings actually do?

For roughly the first century of photography, designers had no practical fix and kept lenses to a handful of elements. In the late 1800s, slightly tarnished lenses were noticed to pass more light than freshly polished ones. Harold Dennis Taylor studied the effect in the 1890s, and in 1935 Zeiss physicist Alexander Smakula worked out how to deposit an even, extremely thin layer on glass in a vacuum chamber. A coating works two ways at once. Its optical properties sit between air and glass, so light makes two smaller jumps instead of one big one. And at a quarter wavelength thick, the reflection off the coating and the reflection off the glass beneath return out of sync and cancel, much like noise-canceling headphones. A single layer fully cancels only one wavelength, usually green, which is why older lenses show a purple sheen. Layering several coatings became multi-coating, a headline feature by the early 1970s.

Newer designs go further. Canon Subwavelength Structure Coating, which debuted in 2008, mimics a moth eye with microscopic wedges that ramp light smoothly from air into glass. No coating stops flare from a sun aimed into the lens, which is why a hood still matters: it blocks light from just outside the frame, the source of much veiling haze.

Why it matters for you

If you adapt vintage glass, you are not fighting a defect. You are choosing a rendering. Softer contrast, glowing highlights, and dramatic flare can work beautifully when they are deliberate, and cinema makers still build uncoated elements for exactly that look. The skill is control. Shoot with a hood, take the front filter off, keep the sun just outside the frame when you want haze and well outside it when you do not, and learn how your specific copy behaves before an important shoot. A lens that flares predictably is a tool. A lens that surprises you is a liability. The difference is testing, and testing is free.
