What the Year's Best Nature Photos Have in Common

November 17, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners, chosen from 27,108 entries from nearly 100 countries, were not won by the rarest animals or the longest lenses. The overall winner is a water lily leaf and its ice replica, photographed a short walk from the photographer's home in Finland. The thread is patience, attention, and looking closely at the ordinary.

What won this year?

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen took the overall title with a photograph of a water lily leaf beside its near-perfect replica: a sheet of ice that had formed on the leaf, slipped off, and settled next to it, veins and all. A plant rarely wins a major competition outright, but the jury could not overlook an image of something that will melt away within days.

The contest drew a record 27,108 entries from nearly 100 countries. Ahonen lives in a wilderness home in Finland and rarely ventures more than a couple of miles from it, spending much of his time behind the camera in his own yard.

What do the category winners share?

Across the categories, the same qualities repeat. The Mammals winner is a giraffe half-lost in fog, a quiet image the judges said revealed its strengths gradually. The Birds winner is an environmental portrait of a woodpecker in Patagonia, chosen for its sense of place. The Animal Portraits winner is a polar bear seen from below, through a hole in the ice.

None of them is the closest, sharpest, or most dramatic version of its subject. The Youth winner, a coyote beneath a great oak in black and white, holds its own against the adult categories on composition alone.

What can you steal from them?

First, shoot close to home. Ahonen found a once-in-a-lifetime frame in his own yard because he was there when the ice arrived and the wind moved the leaves. Second, wait for the moment instead of chasing it: the fog, the last light, the ice slipping off the leaf all reward the photographer who stayed.

Third, look for the frame others walk past. An eagle owl on a church steeple, in plain view of a busy square, went unnoticed by everyone except the photographer who had trained herself to see it.

Why it matters for you

Entries for the 2027 competition open on November 20. The lesson is that you do not need a safari to enter. You need a place you return to, the patience to watch it change, and the discipline to wait for the light. That is the same skill the Arena rewards every night: not the rarest subject, but the best-seen one.
