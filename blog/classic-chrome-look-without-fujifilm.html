A Film Look Is a Recipe, Not a Camera. Recipes Do Not Transfer Cleanly

November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: A Nikon Zf owner compared genuine Fujifilm Classic Chrome from an X-H1 against two Nikon custom picture controls, including the popular CC_NP3 preset, and got close without matching it exactly. Film simulations are recipes applied to different sensors, so chasing another brand's look teaches color control best when you build for your own camera.

What was actually compared?

You do not need Fujifilm to learn what Fujifilm shooters love about Classic Chrome. You need to understand what a film simulation is. Digital Camera World reported on November 26 on a Nikon Zf owner, posting as Odd_Philosopher1286, who also owns a Fujifilm X-H1 and set out to reproduce the X-H1 Classic Chrome look on the Zf. Existing Classic Chrome-style Nikon Flexible Color Picture Controls did not get there, in the owner's judgment, missing the subtle pastel tones and deep shadows. So the owner built a custom picture control and shot the same residential scene three ways: genuine Classic Chrome on the X-H1, the new Zf recipe, and CC_NP3, described as one of the most popular Zf presets for this emulation.

Read the limits before the verdict. The article publishes no numeric recipe, no slider values, and no lens or exposure details, and the comparison rests on a single image. Author Mike Harris is explicit that multiple scenes would be needed to judge properly. That restraint is useful. It keeps the test honest about what one frame can prove.

Why did close still not mean identical?

Because the recipe is only half the rendering. In the comparison as described, genuine Classic Chrome was the coolest of the three, with the highest contrast, deepest shadows, and brightest highlights. The owner's Zf version landed in between, closer than CC_NP3 but warmer and slightly less contrasty, with a more faded feel, less deep blacks, greens carrying a slight yellow tinge, and blues more saturated than CC_NP3. CC_NP3 was the warmest and flattest, with muted color and lifted shadows. The owner is paraphrased as reaching a point where further adjustment stopped getting closer.

The article points to the hardware underneath: the X-H1 uses an X-Trans III sensor with its own green-biased color filtration, while Nikon applies different internal processing. Identical curves would still render differently across the two systems. Commenters in the thread made the companion point that their favorite Zf profiles are often the ones that stop imitating other cameras, with one singling out green-channel differences. Both observations can be true. Emulation is a good exercise. It is a poor destination.

Why it matters for you

Use another brand's look as a teacher, not as a target. If you shoot Nikon, Canon, Sony, or any system without the simulation you admire, name what you actually like in it, in plain terms: cooler balance, deeper shadows, restrained greens, softer highlights. Then build that description in your own picture controls or your editor, test it across skin, foliage, sky, and shade, and keep the version that survives all four. A preset that flatters one street scene and fails on a portrait has taught you its limit. Your camera already has a color character. Learning to steer it on purpose will serve you longer than borrowing someone else's badge for it.
