The Background Decides What Your Aperture Does

November 22, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Aperture alone does not decide how a portrait looks. Distance to your subject, what sits behind them, and the light coming through it matter as much as the f-number. Two frames on the same 35mm lens at f/1.4 can look like they came from different cameras, because of what was behind the couple, not the setting on the lens.

What did the two frames actually show?

Photographer Martin Castein opens an engagement shoot in London with a pair of frames shot on the same 35mm f/1.4, at the same aperture. In the first, the couple is a long way off, and even at f/1.4 almost nothing softens, so it reads like a nice street photo. In the second he is close, the shop fronts go soft, and the sun flares through a tree. About 80 percent of the shoot was done on a 24-70mm f/2.8 zoom, not a prime, because the couple kept walking at slightly different speeds. The zoom let him keep talking and keep them in their rhythm.

Why does distance change the blur more than the f-number?

Because blur is a relationship between three distances, not a property of one setting: how far you are from your subject, how far your subject is from the background, and how wide the lens is. Step back and even f/1.4 barely softens anything. Step in close and f/2.8 melts a street. That is why Castein's 85mm f/1.8, shot around f/2.2, is his favorite for couples: the longer lens sees such a narrow slice of background that a pace or two sideways swaps it for something else entirely. At f/1.2 the plane of focus is so thin that if two faces sit at different distances, one of them goes soft. When a dog started weaving around the couple, Castein stopped down to f/4.5 to keep everyone sharp: at f/2.8 the buildings melt into red brick, while at f/4.5 you can pick out windows and balconies.

When is opening up actually worth it?

When there is something behind your subject worth turning into atmosphere. He points to a backlit frame where the sun comes through a veil and the whole image glows, and credits the light rather than the f-number. His 50mm f/1.2 earns its place for the same reason: wide open it keeps the brick street but turns the highlights into bloom and soft color, so the frame says more about how the day felt than where it was. Light coming through something, or color, is when opening up pays off. With nothing back there worth softening, you just get blur.

Why it matters for you

Stop choosing your aperture by asking how blurry you want the background, and start by asking how much of the place you want in the picture. Try the drill: photograph one pose twice without changing the aperture, once with whatever happens to be behind your subject and once after moving until there is light or color back there, then compare the two at home. Your portraits will start showing the place, the light, and the day, instead of only the blur.
