One Strobe, One White Wall, Two Portraits That Look Nothing Alike

November 16, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer Nathan Elson made two distinct portraits with one Godox strobe and a plain white wall: a moody, shadow-shaped look with an optical snoot, and a clean, bright look with the bare reflector. The difference between them is not more gear. It is distance, angle, and treating shadows as part of the picture.

Why choose hard light when soft light is the safe default?

Soft light gets most of the credit in portrait work, and it earns it: it flatters skin and forgives mistakes. But a single snooted or bare strobe gives you crisp shadows, graphic shapes, and a sense of depth that a big softbox tends to smooth away. If you are working with one strobe and a white wall, hard light is one of the quickest ways to make a small setup look deliberate instead of accidental.

How does one snooted strobe shape a white wall?

For the first look, Elson puts a Godox AD600Pro II inside an optical snoot fitted with framing shutters. The shutters flag the beam so it lands on the model and stays off much of the backdrop, leaving the wall darker and the background shaped. The light sits about 8 feet high, angled down 45 degrees and placed at camera right, so the hard shadow it throws becomes part of the composition. The model tosses a sheer top into the air, and the thin fabric lets the light pass through and paint a soft, translucent shape on the backdrop. At 1/4 power, the strobe meters to f/4 at 1/200 second and ISO 400, shot on a Nikon Zf with a 50mm lens.

Why does moving the light back change everything?

Distance is the most useful lever you have with hard light. Light intensity drops with the square of the distance, so evenness comes down to how far the light is from the closest and farthest parts of your subject. With a strobe 5 feet from a standing person, her head and knees sit at noticeably different distances from the light, and you will see a clear brightness gradient down her body. Pull it back to 15 feet and the falloff from head to toe flattens out. The tradeoff is power: more distance means more output to reach the same aperture, which is where a 600 Ws strobe earns its place.

What changes when the modifier comes off?

The second look strips the modifier off entirely and runs the strobe with its standard reflector. Elson moves the light to camera left, roughly 15 feet from the model, for even light top to bottom. The angle puts the left side of her face into shadow for drama, while her body shadow falls out of frame, leaving a clean white background. Power stays at 1/4, the meter reads f/6 at 1/200 and ISO 100, on an 85mm lens. Same wall, same strobe, same power. A different picture.

Why it matters for you

A one-light limit teaches the two controls that matter most: where the light sits and how far away it is. A second and third light can hide a bad decision about either one. Spend one session with a single strobe and a plain wall, moving the light from 5 to 15 feet, and watch the same portrait change completely.
