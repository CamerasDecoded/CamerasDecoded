A Street Camera Crossed Kyrgyzstan on a Bicycle

November 9, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer Matt Hyde spent 25 days bikepacking across Kyrgyzstan, over 1,000 kilometres and more than twice the elevation of Everest, with a Fujifilm X-E5 and three lenses weighing under three pounds. The street camera kept up because he chose it for the job, not the category, and tested it hard before he left.

What did he actually carry?

Matt Hyde's job on the expedition was documenting the journey, and everything he owned had to be hauled over passes up to 12,400 feet. His kit was a Fujifilm X-E5, an 18-120mm f/4 power zoom, a 35mm f/1.4 for low-light portraits, and a tiny Laowa 10mm pancake for mountain vistas. Camera and three lenses together weighed under three pounds and fit in a waist pack worn while riding. The single zoom mattered most: changing lenses on dusty, wind-swept high passes is a recipe for sensor contamination.

Why a street camera on a mountain crossing?

Conventional wisdom says to bring a ruggedized full-frame body or an ultra-tough Micro Four Thirds setup. Hyde went the other way, and the camera earned its place. On gradients so steep the team pushed their bikes for miles, every ounce mattered. The X-E5's tactile dials allowed instant adjustments to shutter speed, aperture, and focus zones without digging through menus, its subject-detection tracking locked onto fast-moving riders, and the buffer handled long bursts of RAW and JPEG. Fujifilm's Velvia simulation produced vivid, production-ready JPEGs he could transmit during rare moments of cellular connection.

What matters more than the camera you pick?

Hyde did not trust the kit on faith. Before the trip he rigorously tested two bodies and three lenses on domestic shakedown rides to verify performance under load, and he learned to work around the power zoom's slower electronic response before it could cost him a shot. His conclusion is the one worth keeping: modern camera gear is pigeonholed into hyper-specific marketing categories, and understanding your gear's nuances and tailoring its controls to your workflow matters far more than adhering to nominal camera genres.

Why it matters for you

Your next trip does not need a new camera. It needs a kit that never slows you down. One body, one versatile zoom, and a hard test before you leave will take you further than the perfect camera you are still deciding between. The lightest kit you will actually carry beats the best kit you leave behind, and the camera that fits in a waist pack is the camera that is in your hands when the light turns.
