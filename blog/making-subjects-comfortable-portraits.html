Nobody Looks Good Being Watched

October 22, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Stiff portraits come from tense subjects, not wrong settings. Portrait photographer Julia Trotti, writing for Fstoppers, keeps non-models relaxed by opening the session from a comfortable distance, keeping shoots short and fast-paced, and giving loose, open-ended direction. The operator's real job is making the person forget the camera.

Why do non-models freeze up?

Most portrait failures are not exposure failures. They are tension failures. A person who is not a professional model stands in front of your lens feeling watched and unsure, and that self-consciousness settles into their shoulders and stays there. No lens or lighting setup hides it once it arrives.

Distance is the first lever. A waist-up portrait on a wide-angle lens can put you inside a stranger's personal space, which most people reserve for friends and family. Trotti's rule: open from farther away, let trust build, and move in only once the person's posture loosens.

How does she manage distance in a session?

Trotti loves a 35mm prime for environmental portraits, but standing close can overwhelm someone who has never been photographed. When she sees it, she swaps to an 85mm and steps back, restoring the subject's personal space almost instantly — then works back toward the wider look as the person settles in.

With one lens, do the same with your feet: full-body frames from farther back, tighter shots up close, then movement shots with some room again.

What about pacing and direction?

Trotti keeps sessions between 30 minutes and an hour, and she usually has the shots within the first 40 minutes. Past that point you collect more frames instead of better ones. Keep the pace quick: a few frames in one spot, a walk to the next, a switch from standing to sitting.

Direction stays loose. Detailed instructions about chin angle and fingers leave a subject convinced there is only one correct way to stand, and they freeze. Give a general starting point, keep the conversation going about anything other than the shoot, and remind subjects they are free to move. A mood board sent beforehand means the subject arrives knowing what to expect.

Why it matters for you

Your next portrait session is won or lost before you press the shutter. Plan your distance changes, keep a tight shot list and a short clock, and give open-ended direction. The camera skills you already own are enough for a good portrait. The skill you are actually practicing is reading people.
