Turn Any Small Venue Into a Portrait Studio With One Light

October 17, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer Jiggie Alejandrino shot 50 event portraits in a small venue with a single strobe shaped into a book light: a Profoto B10X Plus fired into a reflective panel, then through diffusion fabric, built from two Manfrotto scrim kits. Exposing at 1/320 s, f/2, ISO 100 kills the room's ambient light so the strobe does all the work, and a foam-board budget version gets surprisingly close.

What is a book light?

A book light is two ideas stacked together. The strobe fires into a reflective panel, and the bounced light then passes through a sheet of diffusion fabric before it reaches the subject. It is called a book light because the reflector and the diffusion panel sit at an angle like an open book. The technique started on film and video sets, where gaffers needed a large, even source that would not flare into the lens or throw a hot spot across an actor's face.

For Alejandrino, a brand-launch event in a small venue with about 50 creators and influencers sitting for portraits, the appeal is the same. The session doubled as product work for Audeze headphones, so he needed everything from full-length frames to tight detail shots, all in one corner of the room.

What did his setup actually look like?

The station starts with a hand-painted 7 by 9-foot backdrop from Kate Backdrop. The main light is built from two Manfrotto Pro Scrim kits, one medium and one large: the medium frame carries a reflective panel, and the diffusion panel comes from the large kit. The source is a Profoto B10X Plus, a 500 watt-second strobe with a built-in battery, so there is no separate pack sitting on the floor of an already crowded room.

Fill comes from the other half of the large scrim kit, fitted with a reflective panel and placed opposite the main light. Alejandrino uses it only to open up the shadows: he still wants the light to read as directional, so the reflector lifts the dark side of the face without evening everything out.

Why 1/320, f/2, ISO 100?

That exposure is deliberate: it kills the room's ambient light completely, so the strobe is doing all the work. Bouncing and diffusing costs power, often a couple of stops, which is why the approach pairs so well with a wide aperture and low ISO indoors. His camera was a Sony a1 II with the 50-150mm f/2 GM zoom, which let him go from full-length to tight crops without stepping back into the crowd.

The station also ran a live feed from an Accsoon CineView SE to a monitor on a V-mount battery, so sitters could see themselves while posing, and the camera tethered to a laptop running Capture One and a printer, turning the whole corner into a souvenir booth.

Why it matters for you

The real lesson has nothing to do with scrim kits. Softness depends on how big the light source is relative to the subject, not on what you paid for it. Bouncing a small strobe into a wide panel effectively turns it into a much larger source, and the diffusion layer smooths out the uneven spread a bare reflector leaves behind. A sheet of foam board and a diffusion panel clipped to a stand gets you surprisingly close; the folding frames mostly buy portability.

For events in particular, the broad wrap of a book light is forgiving. Guests are not professional models, and they drift a step forward or turn their shoulders between frames, so a source that stays flattering over a wider range of positions saves you constant corrections. Next time you have one strobe and a tight corner, bounce it big, diffuse it, and kill the ambient. That is the whole trick.
