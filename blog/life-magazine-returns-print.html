Life Magazine Is Coming Back to Print. Study the Photo Essay, Not Just the Photo.

October 29, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Life magazine returns to print on November 23 with a 250-page, large-format first issue priced at 45 dollars. The picture-led weekly that taught America to see the 20th century is back, and its return is a reminder worth taking seriously: single images get likes, but sequences build photographers. Study how a photo essay is put together, not just how a single frame is exposed.

What is actually coming back?

Life announced it will return to print on November 23, in a nod to its very first cover: Margaret Bourke-White's photograph of Montana's Fort Peck Dam from 1936, the year the picture-led weekly began. The first issue carries a Where Are We Now theme, promising a window into an America searching for meaning, with scientists and artists, entrepreneurs and athletes, children and octogenarians, coal miners and drone makers. With more than 120 contributors.

The relaunch comes from Karlie Kloss's Bedford Media, which acquired the rights to Life in 2024. Kloss puts the magazine's appeal simply: what made Life extraordinary was its ability to make the world feel closer. The first issue is large-format, 250 pages, and retails for 45 dollars.

Why did Life matter to photographers?

Life was instrumental in the careers of Alfred Eisenstaedt, Robert Capa, Margaret Bourke-White, and W. Eugene Smith, among others. It peaked in the 1960s selling over eight million copies a week, covering major events, personalities, and everyday realities for a mass audience. The weekly run ended in 1972 as television took the audience, and a monthly version ran until 2000, with occasional special issues since.

What made it matter was not any single photograph. It was the essay: a sequence of pictures edited to carry a story across pages, where a wide establishing frame, a medium moment, and a close detail each did a job the others could not. A photographer could be brilliant in a single frame and still fail the essay, because the essay tested judgment across a set: what to include, what to leave out, and in what order.

What should you steal from it?

Shoot your next outing as a sequence, not a search for one hero frame. Before you go, decide the story in one sentence. Then collect the pieces an editor would need: the wide shot that places the viewer, the medium shot where the action happens, the close detail that only someone standing there would notice, and one quiet frame that lets the set breathe. When you edit, lay them out in order and cut anything that repeats a job another frame already does.

Why it matters for you

Buy the November issue when it lands, and read it the way an editor would: slowly, in order, noticing what each picture does for the one next to it. Then do the same with your own last shoot. The gap between your best single frame and your best sequence is the most useful thing you can measure about your own work.
