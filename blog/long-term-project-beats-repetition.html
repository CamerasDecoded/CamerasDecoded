A Project Is a Boundary, Not a Big Idea

November 6, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: A long-term project cures repetitive shooting by giving every outing a boundary: one place, one route, or one subject, returned to again and again. The constraint does the planning, so you stop hunting for novelty and start noticing what changes. Your first project can be a scouting run; the point is to start.

Why does shooting start to feel repetitive?

Because the routine works. Scout a spot, wait for light, take the frame, go home, and do it again next weekend. That loop produces beautiful single images, but a hard drive full of unrelated pictures rarely adds up to a body of work you can show someone. Landscape photographer Ian Worth describes the same loop in his own shooting: moody, mystical frames, with nothing linking one frame to the next. The missing ingredient is not a better location. It is meaning, and meaning comes from staying with something.

What does a project need to work?

A boundary, not a big idea. Worth tried this before with a series called Between the Hills, covering local towns, villages, and abandoned buildings. Too broad to hold one narrative, but it surfaced smaller stories he could commit to for years. If you have been holding off on a project until you have the perfect concept, treat the first attempt as a scouting run. The pressure drops, and the real subject shows itself while you work.

What kind of boundary works?

One you can state in a single sentence. Worth keeps a running list of ideas on his phone, and the workable ones are all constraints: return to one location every month for twelve months; limit yourself to places within five miles of home; photograph a single subject, one tree or one building, across a full year of weather and light. His own pick is the Golden Road, an ancient track running about seven miles along the ridge of the Preseli Hills in Pembrokeshire, possibly part of a trade route that brought Irish gold to Wessex. He has no idea how long tracing it will take, and treats that uncertainty as the point.

Why it matters for you

A boundary changes the edit first: a quiet frame of a gate or a farmhouse that would never survive a cull on its own can earn a place when it moves the story forward. Pick one boundary this week and write the brief in a single sentence. Four returns to the same place teach you more than four new locations: the fourth visit is where the photograph only you could have made appears.
