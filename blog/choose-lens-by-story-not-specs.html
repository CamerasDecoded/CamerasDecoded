Choose Your Next Lens by the Story, Not the Spec Sheet

November 7, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Professional photographers choose lenses by the story they want to tell, not by the job each focal length is supposed to do. Photographer Brenda Bergreen's framework asks four questions: what is the story, what visual effect do you want, how close should the viewer feel, and how close can you actually get?

Why do the usual lens rules fall short?

Choosing a lens feels technical: wide for landscapes, long for wildlife, something in between for the rest. Photographer Brenda Bergreen argues that way of thinking misses the point. After fifteen years as a professional, she says lens selection has become less about numbers and more about visual intent: the same scene can produce several different photographs, depending on where you stand and which lens you pick.

What does story-first lens choice look like?

Start with one question: what do you want the photograph to say? A mountain biker portrait can focus on the rider, the landscape, or the relationship between the two. An 85mm lens tightens the composition and simplifies the background, putting the attention on the rider. A 24mm or 35mm lens, used from close range, includes the trail, trees, and rocks. Step farther back and make the rider small, and the emphasis shifts to the landscape. The subject stays the same. The photograph tells a different story.

How does a lens place the viewer?

Once the story is clear, the next decision is how the image should look and feel. Wider lenses include more of the environment and make an environmental portrait with a sense of place. Longer focal lengths isolate a subject and cut distractions. Position matters as much as the lens: close with a wide angle exaggerates the foreground; back with a long lens compresses the scene.

Lens choice also decides where the viewer belongs. Close with a wide lens, and the image feels immersive, as if the viewer is standing inside the scene. Farther away, the viewer becomes an observer watching from a distance. Neither is better. The effects combine too: isolate a person while keeping enough background to show where they are.

Why it matters for you

Bergreen's framework comes down to four questions: what is the story, what visual effect do you want, how close should the viewer feel, and how close can you actually get? Ask them before your next lens purchase, and ask them with the lens you already own. Shoot one subject at three focal lengths and watch the story change. The best lens is not necessarily the longest or the sharpest. It is the one that helps you make the photograph you had in mind.
