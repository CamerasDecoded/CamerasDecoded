Most Photography Rules Are Old Workarounds. Learn When to Break Them.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Most photography rules were workarounds for technical limits that no longer exist. The 1/focal-length shutter rule predates in-body stabilization, and keep-ISO-low predates modern sensors and AI denoise. The test for any rule is simple: ask what problem it was built to solve, and whether your camera, your software, or your audience still has that problem.

Why do the old rules feel wrong now?

The rules you were handed were mostly workarounds for limits that have moved. Keeping your shutter speed at 1/focal length dates back to 35mm film, before stabilization let you handhold several stops slower. Keeping ISO low made sense on early digital sensors; modern sensors and AI tools like Lightroom's Denoise, added in 2023, have pushed the usable range much further out.

Even framing habits have shifted with how pictures get viewed. Instagram moved its profile grid to taller 3:4 previews in early 2025, which favors compositions that survive a vertical crop. When a rule feels rigid, ask what problem it was built to solve and whether your camera, your software, or your audience still has that problem.

What does breaking the rules actually look like?

Wedding photographers Mark and Brenda Bergreen work through the noon-light problem directly. The best view of the Matterhorn might show up at 11 a.m., and walking past it because the sun is high makes little sense. They use a polarizing filter to deepen the sky, turn so the sun hits the scene from the side, and go for a silhouette to simplify the frame. They cannot schedule around the sun, and the photos still have to be good.

On settings, they reach for f/16 for sunstars, f/5.6 to f/8 for group shots, and f/11 for architecture they want sharp throughout. A 1-second exposure turns an ocean view into something closer to a painted palette. For ISO they will push to 3,200 or 6,400 for stars and accept 12,800 in a dark, chaotic scene, since a grainy frame beats a missed moment. They also crop in post, carry a cheaper lens into the rain, and skip UV filters for a polarizer. A few rules survive, including a firm stance against tilting the camera to rescue a boring frame.

Why it matters for you

Rules are training wheels, not laws, and the difference between a beginner breaking a rule and a photographer breaking one is intention. Learn each rule well enough to name the problem it solves, then test it against what your camera can actually do. The next time a rule tells you not to shoot, ask whether it is protecting the picture or just an old limitation. Shoot the noon light. Push the ISO. Keep the frame that works.
