Why the Best Photos Leave a Question Unanswered

November 13, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: The photos people remember share one trait: they leave a question unanswered. Filmmaker Max Kent argues a strong image gives the viewer one clear anchor, a tense face, a shadow, fresh footprints, and one deliberate absence, so curiosity does the work. The viewer fills in the rest, and that is what makes the frame stick.

What is the idea?

Kent demonstrates it with a cardboard box. He tells you the contents are cool, then refuses to open it. That irritation is the point: curiosity is the gap between what you know and what you want to know, an idea economist George Loewenstein laid out in 1994.

Think of the average photo online: a person smiling, a landmark, a pretty street. Each is finished in a second. The images Kent admires work the other way. He points to Sam Stone, a photographer he calls a master of this, whose pictures leave you wondering: whose footprints are those, whose shadow falls across the wall?

How do you build it into a photo?

The recipe is one readable anchor paired with one deliberate absence. Before you press the shutter, decide what the frame will answer and what it will hold back. Crop out the thing a person is reacting to. Shoot a figure from behind. Let a shadow stand in for its owner.

It is the iceberg principle: leave most of the story under the surface. Kent's other point is that less usually carries more. The whole tower, the whole square, the whole vista makes a nice frame, but it rarely stays with anyone for years. It is the box handed over already open.

Where does Cameras Decoded stand on this?

Kent is right, and the one caution is his own: pictures made confusing for the sake of confusion miss the goal. The gap only works when the anchor is genuinely readable, a tense face, a crisp shadow, fresh footprints. Without that, there is no curiosity, only noise.

Our addition is a test. If a stranger who does not know you pauses on the frame, the gap is working. If only you care, it is a private memory, not a photograph. That is the same test blind voting runs every night in the Arena.

Why it matters for you

On your next walk, decide what each frame will answer and what it will hold back. Photograph less of the whole scene and more of the part that makes someone ask what happened just outside the frame. At the end, Kent opens the box, and it is empty.
