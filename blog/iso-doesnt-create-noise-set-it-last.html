ISO Doesn't Create Noise. Set It Last.

November 17, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Noise comes from too little light reaching the sensor, not from the ISO dial. ISO only amplifies the signal already captured, so underexposing to protect a low ISO and brightening later gives a noisier file than a correct exposure at high ISO. Set shutter speed and aperture first, then let ISO land where the exposure needs it.

Where does noise actually come from?

Light arrives at the sensor as particles, and in dim light those particles land unevenly. Photographer Pierre T. Lambert compares it to rain on pavement: in a downpour every patch gets soaked evenly, but at the start of a shower one spot catches three drops while the next catches none. That unevenness between neighboring pixels is the noise you see.

ISO adds no light at all. It only amplifies the signal the sensor already collected, the way turning up a quiet recording makes its hiss louder. Lambert's test: one frame at ISO 500 brightened five stops in post, another at ISO 16,000 left untouched. Zoomed in, the pushed ISO 500 file was actually worse.

Why does underexposing to protect ISO backfire?

The light you never capture is gone. Lambert's own shoot makes the case: photographing horseback riding on a beach, his shutter was still at 1/2000 of a second when a walking subject needed only 1/500, so auto ISO pushed to 16,000. The mistake was the shutter speed, not the ISO.

High ISO still has real costs. An image shot two stops dark at a high ISO and corrected later falls apart; a correctly exposed frame at the same setting holds up. And raising ISO keeps brightening the image until the highlights turn pure white, which no slider brings back.

How should you actually set ISO?

Set it last. Shutter speed comes first, chosen for the motion in the scene, then aperture for depth of field, and ISO lands wherever the exposure needs it. Shooting hikers in headlamps, Lambert needed 1/50 of a second, which pushed his camera to ISO 25,600. A noisy photo can be cleaned up. A blurry one cannot.

His recommendation is auto ISO with an upper limit, 51,200 on his Sony a7 V. The homework is simple: shoot one scene at every ISO, doubling from 100, stop where the noise bothers you, then set your limit a notch or two higher.

Why it matters for you

Most photographers cap their ISO far too low and pay with blurry frames and underexposed files. The fear of grain costs more good photos than grain ever will. Learn your ceiling, set auto ISO to it, and stop thinking about the number.
