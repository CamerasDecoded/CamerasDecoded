Seven Small Habits That Can Break Your Camera

November 13, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Landscape photographer Michael Scott lists seven habits that damage camera gear: carrying a camera on an extended tripod, changing lenses in the wind, moving gear from cold to hot, trusting tripod locks unchecked, treating weather sealing as waterproof, leaving the hood in the bag, and rushing when the light turns good. The damage comes from routine handling, not dramatic accidents.

What breaks gear most often?

Not the dramatic accident. Scott's central point is that gear usually gets damaged during routine handling. Extended tripod legs catch on branches or knock rocks while you walk, and the camera can slip if the ball head or quick-release plate is not secure. A lens change outdoors exposes the sensor chamber to dust, sand, sea spray, and insects, with the wind carrying all of it straight inside.

The quieter risks are temperature and trust. Moving a camera from an air-conditioned car into hot, humid weather can cause condensation inside the equipment, and moisture inside a lens or body causes problems beyond a fogged front element. A tripod is only as stable as its weakest lock, and loose locks can collapse and send an expensive camera onto rocks.

Which habits are the cheapest to fix?

Most of them cost nothing. Remove the camera before moving; if carrying everything together is unavoidable, put the lens hood on, point the lens downward, and check the ball head is tight. Point the camera down while changing lenses, and find shelter when conditions are bad.

For temperature changes, seal the camera in a plastic bag before moving into warmer air and leave it there while it adjusts. Check tripod locks regularly, and consider claw-style or spiked feet for grip on rocks, sand, and creek beds. And use the lens hood: it takes the impact from a bump before the front of the lens does.

What is the one habit that causes the others?

Rushing for good light. When photographers hurry to catch a sunrise or sunset, they forget every precaution at once: a tripod opened unchecked, a lens changed in the wind, equipment moved unsecured. Scott's fix is to arrive early and plan compositions before the light turns interesting, so a sudden burst of light does not turn into a frantic scramble.

Why it matters for you

A repair bill lasts longer than the light does. The most expensive habit here is the one that feels like saving time. Build the checks into your setup routine now, while the light is boring, so you do not think about them when it turns spectacular. The shot is fleeting. A broken lens is not.
