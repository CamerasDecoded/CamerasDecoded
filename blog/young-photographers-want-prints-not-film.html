Young Photographers Are Not Quitting Digital. They Are Printing Again.

November 11, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: A survey commissioned by AgfaPhoto found that 8 in 10 Europeans aged 18 to 35 print their photos, and half of those printers do it for a retro, vintage look. The headline reads like a film revival, but the study measured printing, not camera choice. The real story is that young photographers want their pictures to be tangible.

What did the survey actually find?

The survey, commissioned by ecommerce platform AgfaPhoto, questioned 4,000 people in Germany, France, the UK, and Italy. Seven in 10 print their photos or turn them into a keepsake, and among 18- to 35-year-olds that rises to 8 in 10. Of those young printers, half said they print for a retro, vintage aesthetic, and 40 percent said they print to show images at family occasions.

Read the numbers carefully, because the headline oversells them. The 50 percent figure describes young people who already print, not young people as a whole, and the study measured printing, not camera choice: 35 percent of all respondents use a smartphone as their main camera. The article itself urges a pinch of salt, noting that AgfaPhoto has not publicly released the survey dataset or clarified its methodology.

Why does the retro look matter?

The article argues the resurgence goes beyond the vintage aesthetic popularized on Instagram. It cites US editor Hillary Grigonis's conversation with a Fujifilm executive: for some, analog is a way out of a culture where a photo's worth is measured in likes and comments. Julien Lelarge, marketing director at GT Company, an AgfaPhoto brand licensee, puts it as a desire to give certain memories special meaning and to make them tangible.

That is the thread worth pulling. The film camera is not really the point. The point is that a photo you can hold cannot be scrolled past, and the act of choosing one frame to print forces the same deliberateness film used to impose.

Why it matters for you

You do not need to buy a film camera to learn this lesson. The discipline is in the finishing, not the format. Pick your ten best frames from the last month and print them, small is fine, and put them where you will actually see them. A print on the wall tells you more about your own work than a hundred likes, because you have to live with it. Mirrorless shooters can borrow everything film teaches about slowing down and choosing, and keep the camera they own. The takeaway is not to switch systems. It is to make your photos exist somewhere other than a feed.
