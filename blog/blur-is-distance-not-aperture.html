Background Blur Is About Distance, Not Just Aperture

November 1, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Background blur is geometry before it is glass. Move closer to your subject, give the background room behind it, and use a longer focal length, and even a kit lens at f/5.6 will soften a scene. A fast prime helps, but it is the last lever to reach for, not the first.

What did the kit lens prove?

Photographer Jason Vong, in a video covered by Fstoppers, takes apart the idea that blurry backgrounds require an f/1.8 or f/1.2 lens. He shoots an 18mm kit lens at f/3.5 on an APS-C body, and two frames at identical settings look completely different because he walks closer to the subject for the second one. He then zooms to 50mm and steps back to keep a full-body framing; the kit lens narrows to f/5.6, and the background still softens. That is also why long telephoto lenses at f/6.3 or f/8 can produce smooth backgrounds. The space behind your subject counts just as much: a flat wall stays dull at any f-stop, while open depth changes the whole look.

Why does shooting wide open go soft?

Wide open is not automatically sharp. Budget and vintage primes are often soft at their widest aperture, especially away from the center, and stopping down to somewhere between f/2.8 and f/4 usually cleans that up. The other cause is depth of field itself: at f/1.8 the plane of focus is so thin that rocking slightly forward or back can move your subject out of focus even when the camera signals a lock. Vong's fix is simple: start at 1/125 of a second or faster, switch to burst mode, hold steady, and fire several frames. And he argues against shooting wide open every time. An 85mm portrait at f/1.8 against a city skyline loses its sense of place; at f/5.6 or f/8, the location reads clearly.

What shutter speeds should you start from?

Vong's baselines are 1/125 for still portraits, 1/250 for moving people, 1/500 for kids and animals, and 1/1000 or faster for sports and wildlife. The old rule of one over your focal length dates back to 35mm film and prints viewed at ordinary sizes; on a high-resolution file, a tiny bit of shake that never showed on a 4 by 6 print becomes visible at 100 percent. Treat the old rule as a floor and go faster when sharpness matters, while stabilization lets you hold slower speeds on static subjects. For unpredictable light such as concerts, he relies on auto ISO with a ceiling around 6,400.

Why it matters for you

Before you spend on a faster lens, spend an afternoon on distance. Step closer, step back with a longer focal length, and put real space behind your subject, and watch what your current lens already does. You will save money, you will miss fewer shots to razor-thin focus, and you will start choosing aperture for the picture you want, whether that means melting a background away or keeping the place in the frame.
