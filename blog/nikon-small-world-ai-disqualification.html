Nikon Stripped a $3,000 Win Over AI, and It Changes the Rules for Everyone

October 11, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Nikon disqualified Dr Ning Xu's winning entry in its Small World in Motion competition for violating its generative-AI rule, then promoted the runner-up. Xu admitted using AI in post-processing but said he did not break the rules. The case shows contest AI rules are now being enforced, not just written.

What actually happened?

The winning video in Nikon's Small World in Motion competition was disqualified after re-review. Nikon told PetaPixel the entry did not comply with the competition's generative AI rules, and adjusted the rankings. The prize went to Nguyen Nam Nhat for a video of a tiny roundworm and a single-celled Dileptus, with Benedikt Pleyer's jellyfish larvae moving to second and Dr Andrew Moore's synchronized cell division to third.

The disqualified video, by National University of Singapore researcher Dr Ning Xu, was presented as showing the abnormal beating of airway cilia in a child with a rare respiratory disorder. Scientists raised alarms on LinkedIn after spotting inconsistencies: structures that popped in and out of existence, and cilia that did not match known sizes. Xu admitted using AI in post-processing but denied breaking the rules, which state plainly that AI-generated videos are not permitted and that the sponsor may request the original video for verification.

Why does a microscopy contest matter to a mirrorless shooter?

Because the enforcement pattern will not stay in science. Contests across photography have been rewriting rules around AI for two years, and most of them were honor systems. This is one of the first times a major brand has publicly stripped a win and paid the runner-up, and it sets the template: re-review after public concern, adjust rankings, and revise procedures.

The detail worth noticing is what would have settled it. Other scientists said the dispute could have been resolved by sharing the raw grayscale video. In other words, the safety net is the same as it has always been: the unedited file. Your camera's raw files are not just your archive; in a world where judges can no longer trust pixels, they are your receipt.

Why it matters for you

Read the AI clause of any competition you enter before you touch a slider, because basic post-processing and generative editing are now different legal categories. Tools like generative fill, AI subject replacement, and video frame interpolation sit on the wrong side of most contest lines, even when the result looks plausible. Keep your raw files organized and unmodified, and when in doubt, assume judges can ask for them.
