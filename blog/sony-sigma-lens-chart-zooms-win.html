Zooms Won the Lens Charts. Here Is What That Means for Your Next Lens

November 24, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Yodobashi's latest lens sales chart is nine zooms and one prime. Photographers buy versatility first: the Sony 24-70mm f/2.8 GM II leads, and Sigma's new 50-120mm f/2.8 for APS-C cameras appears three times, once per mount. The lesson for learners is simple. A good zoom teaches you every focal length before a prime teaches you one.

What does the lens chart actually show?

Yodobashi Camera, one of Japan's largest photo retailers, shared its ten best-selling lenses for the second half of October, and the pattern is hard to miss. Nine of the ten are zooms. The Sony FE 24-70mm f/2.8 GM II leads the list, followed by the Tamron 25-200mm f/2.8-5.6 Di III VXD G2, the Canon RF 70-200mm f/2.8L IS USM Z, and the Canon RF 100-400mm f/5.6-8 IS USM. Three Sony G Master lenses make the top ten, alongside professional glass from Canon and Nikon. This is one retail chain in one market, and the industry is short on public sales figures, so a Japanese retailer's chart is among the richest data available. But the trend is not new: almost all of Map Camera's ten best-selling lenses of 2025 were zooms as well.

What is the one prime doing there?

The lone prime on the list is the brand-new Sony FE 600mm f/6.3 GM OSS, a super-telephoto that is also the priciest lens in the top ten. It is a specialist tool for wildlife and sports, bought by photographers who already know exactly what they need. That is the point. Primes dominate the conversation about image quality, and zooms dominate the cash register. Working photographers spend on the lens that covers the most situations, and the prime comes later, for the one job it does better than anything else.

What is the real news for learners?

Sigma's brand-new 50-120mm f/2.8 DC OS Contemporary for APS-C mirrorless cameras. It appears on the chart three times, once for each mount, because Yodobashi tracks mounts separately. The Fujifilm X-mount version is the most popular, outselling the Canon RF and Sony E-mount versions, and Sigma has not announced an L-mount version yet. A constant f/2.8 zoom in the 50-120mm range is the classic portrait and event workhorse, roughly 75-180mm in full-frame terms, and this is the first time APS-C shooters have had one at this price and size. It is the lens that lets a crop-sensor camera do the job people usually assume needs full frame.

Why it matters for you

Buy the versatile lens first. A good zoom is a school: one lens lets you try 24mm, 50mm, 70mm, and 200mm in a single afternoon, and you learn which focal lengths you actually reach for before you spend prime money on one of them. The chart is a mirror of what working photographers do with their own money, and they buy the lens that covers the most ground. When you know which end of the zoom you live on, then buy the prime that does that job better. Until then, versatility is the lesson, and the Sigma 50-120mm is the proof that the lesson now comes in an APS-C size.
