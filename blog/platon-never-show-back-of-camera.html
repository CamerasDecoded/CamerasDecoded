Why Platon Never Shows Anyone the Back of His Camera

November 11, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Portrait photographer Platon never shows a sitter the back of his camera, not even heads of state. He does not tether, keeps no screen in the room, and never reviews frames during a shoot, because reviewing turns a session into constant self-editing. His trust comes from going first, telling sitters honestly how he feels, not from proving the shot worked.

What does Platon actually do?

Platon has photographed presidents, dictators, and pop stars with the same Hasselblad, white seamless backdrop, and apple box for decades. His digital rules are stricter than most: no tethering, no screen in the room, no reviewing frames during the shoot. When a subject asks to see, he says no, and he says it to heads of state too.

That refusal works because he earns trust another way: he goes first, telling sitters honestly how he feels that day so they can meet him halfway. His stories show what that concentration buys. Hugo Chavez gave him about 30 seconds at the United Nations and he got one frame. Adele gave him three seconds, and her face changed the moment her newborn son squeezed her ankle. For Time's Person of the Year cover, he waited six hours for one frame.

Why keep the setup the same every time?

The constant setup has a purpose: the apple box, the seamless, the lights, and the camera stay the same, so the only thing that changes is the person. On set, his decisions are about the subject. If the eyes carry the story, he moves in close; if it is posture or the way someone holds their hands, he goes wide.

What does this have to do with AI?

Platon makes a broader argument: as AI erodes trust in what people see, real human experience becomes more valuable. In 2023 the Leica M11-P became the first camera to embed Content Credentials, a signed record of an image's origin and edits, and Sony has since added similar features to several bodies. Those tools prove a file was not fabricated. They cannot make a viewer feel that two people actually connected in a room, and that feeling is what Platon builds his method around.

Why it matters for you

If you have ever lost momentum by turning the camera around to show someone a frame, you know the cost of chimping: the session becomes a review meeting and the sitter starts performing for the screen. Try one portrait session with image review off and the screen facing away from your subject. Keep the setup constant, one light, one backdrop, one lens, so the only variable is the person. The portrait gets better when the session belongs to them, not to your histogram.
