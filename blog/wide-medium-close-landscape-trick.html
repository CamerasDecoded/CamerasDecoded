Shoot Every Landscape Three Times: Wide, Medium, Close

October 3, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Landscape photographer Jason Friend borrows a filmmaker's coverage sequence for stills: one establishing wide, one medium frame on the subject, one close-up detail. The constraint, shoot all three before leaving a location, spreads your risk on uncertain mornings and keeps you looking longer, and the close-up is usually the frame that surprises you.

How does the sequence work?

You arrive at a beach at sunrise and nearly every frame is the widest view your lens allows, which leaves most of the location unphotographed. Filmmakers cover a scene with a wide shot, a medium shot, and a close-up, and Jason Friend borrows that sequence for a sunrise shoot at Old Hartley Bay on the Northumberland coast. He opens on a Nikon Z7 and a 14-30mm f/4 S, finding a curve of stone the 14mm end makes dramatic, the lighthouse kept tiny in the distance on purpose.

For the middle frame he switches to a 24-120mm f/4 S and treats 35mm to 70mm as normal. Now the lighthouse is the subject. The close-up is the hardest to find. He lands on a portrait-orientation frame of pebbles and sculpted green rock, adds a polarizer to cut the reflected sky off the wet surfaces, and focus brackets by hand for a later Photoshop blend. By morning's end he has worked from 14mm to roughly 90mm, the same ground a film crew covers with a wide prime, a 50mm, and something near 85mm.

What happens when the morning fights back?

The clouds move faster than he would like, and rain arrives uninvited. He adds an ND filter, pushes the exposure beyond one second at f/14, and leans into a mood he never planned for. His view on realism is plain: he would not take that liberty with wildlife, but a landscape is his interpretation, so smoothing the water is fair game. He even takes a hard fall on the rocks, camera safely packed away. The sequence survives because the constraint keeps him working instead of packing up.

Why it matters for you

Set a rule before you leave the car: one frame at the wide end of your zoom, one near 50mm, one at the long end, and you do not pack up until you have all three. It spreads your risk on uncertain mornings. If the sky goes flat, a close-up of wet stone barely notices. It also fits how photos are seen now. Instagram raised its carousel limit from 10 images to 20 in 2024, and a sequence that moves from wide view to subject to texture gives someone a reason to keep swiping.
