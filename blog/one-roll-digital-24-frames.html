Shoot Like You Only Have 24 Frames and Watch What Changes

November 14, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Jason Friend spent a day treating a Nikon Zf like a single 24-exposure roll of ISO 100 black-and-white film: no reviewing shots, no autofocus, no stabilization. He found the exercise copied film's frustrations better than its pleasures, but the discipline of committing to a frame before you shoot is worth borrowing.

What was the experiment?

On the North Yorkshire coast, Jason Friend set himself a strict rule: treat a Nikon Zf as though it holds a single 24-exposure roll of ISO 100 black-and-white film. He mounted an old Nikkor 28-105mm zoom through two adapters, giving him no autofocus and a manual aperture ring, and banned himself from reviewing shots, zooming in to check focus, or using stabilization.

The first frames went wrong in a way any Zf owner will recognize. Left in aperture priority, the top shutter speed dial does nothing, so every careful adjustment he made with a strong ND filter attached was ignored. The reason for the experiment is relatable: his film camera had sat at home for six weeks next to a roll of Fujifilm Neopan Acros 100 II, because each frame costs real money.

What did the limit actually teach?

With two frames left and plenty of card space, he had to choose between a shot he had planned and a new composition of the village houses he liked better. He took the new one. That is the whole lesson in miniature: a limit forces a decision, and the decision is the photograph.

The black-and-white viewfinder preview is not authentic to film, but he still recommends it to anyone learning monochrome, because it shows directly how colors turn into tones. In the end he says he will not use this combination again. The day mostly showed how much easier digital makes the job, and with real film he would have spent longer over each shot and its metering, with print film's exposure latitude as a cushion.

Should you try it?

Try it once, but borrow one limit at a time. Cap a walk at 24 frames, keep the rear screen off until you get home, or shoot one prime all day. Any single limit forces you to commit to a composition before you shoot, and that habit carries over when the limits are gone.

Do not fetishize the hardship. The point is the seeing, not the suffering. Friend copied film's frustrations more successfully than its pleasures, and that is useful information too: the discipline is worth keeping, the inconvenience is not.

Why it matters for you

The delete key is free, so nothing forces you to decide before you shoot. That is digital's gift and its trap. A self-imposed limit is a training tool, not a lifestyle, and one roll's worth of deliberate frames will teach you more about your own seeing than a thousand sprayed ones.
