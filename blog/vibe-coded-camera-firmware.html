Vibe Coding Has Reached Your Camera

November 22, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: AI-assisted tools are now letting photographers decompile and modify their cameras' firmware, adding features manufacturers never shipped: open-gate recording on Sony bodies, an XPan mode on the Panasonic S9, continuous autofocus on the original Hasselblad X2D. The barrier to entry has collapsed. So has the safety net.

What is vibe coding, and what is it doing to cameras?

Rewriting a camera's firmware was once a job for a handful of specialists. The Canon Hack Development Kit, from around 2006, and Magic Lantern, which grew out of the Canon 5D Mark II, proved it could be done, but the work stayed rare. In the mirrorless era it nearly stopped. That changed in the past month or two. AI coding tools have become good at decompiling firmware, deriving source code from published software, a job that used to take a human enormous time and expertise. People with no software background are now modifying their cameras, and the projects are spreading fast.

What features are people actually adding?

Mostly, the features manufacturers supposedly left out. Open-gate recording has been added to Sony, Nikon, and Blackmagic cameras, and the Sony a7 IV version is now available to download. The a7S III has gained variable shutter and open-gate recording. There is a 65:24 XPan mode for the Panasonic S9, highlight-weighted metering for Fujifilm bodies, and continuous autofocus and video for the original Hasselblad X2D. Other projects go further. Caduceus, built for the OM-3 and OM-5 Mark II, adds an advanced color editor for out-of-camera JPEGs and film simulation presets. And OM-1 Mark II features have been ported back to the original OM-1, the model OM System implied could not run them.

What are the risks?

Real ones, and they start before you install anything. In the United States the Digital Millennium Copyright Act prohibits circumventing the technological measures that control access to a copyrighted work, which is what firmware modification amounts to. A camera company told DPReview that within the warranty period you may not be covered, and outside it parts may no longer be available if the firmware damages hardware. At least one developer claims to have bricked a Fujifilm X30 in the attempt, and r/SonyAlpha has banned discussion of reverse-engineered firmware outright. A manufacturer validates a firmware change so it can stand behind it. A vibe-coded project may be maintained by someone who could not fix a bug if their language model could not.

Why it matters for you

Your camera is a computer with a lens attached, and understanding its software is becoming part of the craft, not a hobby for engineers. You do not need to install anyone's firmware on your only body. But learn what your camera's software actually does, because the features you assumed were hardware limits are, increasingly, software decisions. Learn the tool deeply before you rewrite it.
