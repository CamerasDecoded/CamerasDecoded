Take Fewer Photos. See More.

October 10, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer James Popsys used to fire 1,000 to 2,000 frames a day on trips, until he started asking whether the habit amounted to cheating. On the east coast of Greenland he deliberately cut back to around 250 frames a day. The trip felt better, and so did the take. A digital frame costs nothing, but culling thousands of images every night is its own unpaid job.

What does an overshot day actually cost you?

Burst shooting costs nothing per frame, so after a big day somewhere new it is easy to come home with thousands of files and an evening of culling ahead. That math feels free until you notice where your attention went. Popsys separates taking photos from photography: pressing the shutter sits in the middle of a longer process, with curiosity, anticipation, and inspiration on one side and pride, or more often despair, on the other.

Time is the first cost. Culling thousands of images every night after a full day outside is its own job, and anyone who has done it on a trip knows how fast it eats the evening. The second cost is presence. The longer your eye stays glued to the viewfinder, the less time you spend scanning the scene for something more interesting than what you are already pointed at, and the less time you spend simply being somewhere remarkable.

Why is every camera pushing the opposite direction?

Camera makers have spent the last few years making volume easier. Electronic shutters on current mirrorless bodies reach 30 frames per second or more, and pre-capture modes buffer frames before you even finish pressing the shutter, so one press can produce dozens of files. Those tools earn their place in wildlife and sports, where a wingbeat or a peak moment lasts a fraction of a second.

In landscape, street, and travel work, the deciding factor is usually where you stand and what you include in the frame, and frame rate has no answer for that. The spray-and-pray habit leaks from the genres that need it into the ones where it quietly replaces thinking.

What does shooting less look like in practice?

Popsys borrowed a discipline from film. He took the question to the east coast of Greenland, around Tasiilaq, an area he first visited eight years earlier and remembers as the first place that felt like another planet. Places that remote make him want to overshoot; this time his average dropped to around 250 frames a day. That is still a lot, he says, but the trip felt better and he is happier with what he brought home.

You can borrow the same discipline without changing any gear. Switch the drive mode to single shot for a walk, or give yourself a fixed number of frames per location, the way a roll of 120 film gives you somewhere between 8 and 16 exposures depending on format. A budget moves the decision to before the exposure, while you can still walk ten steps to the left. Without one, the choice happens later at a laptop, picking between forty nearly identical frames.

Slowing down did not make Popsys immune to mistakes, and he proves it with his own frames, including a roof line sitting far too close to a ridge line behind it. He points out that the collision is exactly the kind of detail he would miss far more often while firing frame after frame, and that leaving room to catch it in the moment is the whole point. He credits a comment he once read under a filmmaking video: a teacher who tells students they can make any decision they like, as long as the viewer believes they did it on purpose.

Why it matters for you

If you are learning, volume feels like progress and culling feels like homework, and that pairing quietly trains you to shoot first and see later. Flip it for one outing. Set a number before you leave, switch to single shot, and make every frame a decision you can defend. You will walk more, look more, and come home with fewer files and more keepers. The camera companies will keep selling you frames per second. Your job is to know when the story needs one frame instead of thirty.
