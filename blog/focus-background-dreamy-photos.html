Focus on the Background and Your Subject Starts to Disappear

November 7, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Focusing on the background while your subject goes soft is one of the simplest ways to make a photograph feel dreamy. Use autofocus lock or back-button focus to hold focus on the scene behind your subject, shoot around f/3.5 to f/4 at 35mm or 50mm, and keep the figure recognizable so the viewer can still read it.

What is an out-of-focus subject?

Nearly everything you learn about focus says keep the subject sharp. Photographer Max Kent reverses it. The person in front of the lens goes soft while the scene behind them stays crisp, and the image turns dreamy without slow shutters, blue hour, or anything smeared on the lens.

The figure keeps its shape, so the viewer still knows it is a person, yet it feels barely there. Most of us made frames like this by accident when we started. The deliberate version takes a few choices before the shutter, and no new gear.

How do you put focus on the background?

Manual focus is the most direct route: set the lens on the scene behind your subject and shoot. Kent usually stays in autofocus, focuses on the background, holds the lock, and recomposes. Let go and the camera behaves normally, so you can switch between this look and conventional frames on one walk. Back-button focus works too: once focus leaves the shutter button, it stays where you put it.

Mirrorless autofocus fights you here: face and eye detection jump to a person the moment one enters the frame. A single small focus point with subject detection off makes the camera cooperative.

What settings make the look work?

Focal length shapes the result. Kent shoots almost entirely at 35mm, getting physically closer to the subject. At 50mm and longer the effect comes more easily, and the compression adds to the strangeness. He would not go wider than 35mm: wide lenses hide the depth-of-field falloff the effect depends on.

Aperture is where most people stumble: opening the lens all the way turns the subject into a shapeless smear. Kent aims for f/3.5 to f/4, which keeps the figure recognizable while still showing the depth. At 100mm, try f/5.6. The same idea works through blurred walls and fence gaps, pushing the eye toward the sharp part of the scene.

Why it matters for you

The look has a long history: Pictorialists chased softness on purpose a century ago, and diffusion filters are still popular for the same reason. This version costs nothing: only where you place your focus point. Try it on your next outing with whatever is already in your bag. The accident you used to delete is a technique now.
