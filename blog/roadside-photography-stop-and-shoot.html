The Best Photos on Your Commute Are the Ones You Drive Past

November 12, 2026 · 4 min read
In short: Photographer Rick Bebbington spent two days shooting only what he could see from the road near his home in England. His rule is one any learner can steal: pin the spots you drive past, return when the light is right, and spend 80 percent of your time on your normal work and 20 percent on experiments like this.

What did the experiment actually prove?

Bebbington set himself a constraint: over two days near his home in England, he would photograph only what he could see from the road, working with a Fujifilm X-E5 and X-H2S. No planned shots, no destinations. Everything he pointed his camera at was a bonus.

The frames are ordinary subjects made good by attention. A yellow Volkswagen Beetle under a tree turning orange, shot on the second morning once the car beside it had gone. A man walking a small dog along a canal towpath. Some gambles failed, but the failures cost nothing but a drive.

How do you turn a commute into a scouting route?

The part worth copying is not the shooting, it is the returning. Bebbington marks spots on a map, so a drive becomes something he can revisit. Apps like PhotoPills show where the sun rises and sets from any spot, so a pin dropped on a gray Tuesday tells you which afternoon puts the light where you want it.

A saved list in Google Maps or a notes file on your phone, filled in each time you drive somewhere routine, slowly becomes a scouting record of your own area. Safety comes first: find the nearest safe place to pull over, even if it puts you a long way from what you wanted.

What is our position on this?

Bebbington's experiment is worth doing, but not as a weekend project. Make it a habit attached to something you already do. Every learner has a route they drive weekly. That route is a standing assignment with free repetition, and repetition is how an eye gets trained. Pin three spots this week. Return to one when the light is right.

Bebbington closes with a ratio: about 80 percent of your time on the work you normally make, and 20 percent on experiments like this. We agree, and we would put the roadside habit inside the 20 percent, because paying attention matters more than any camera or edit.

Why it matters for you

Most learners believe they need somewhere new to make something worth keeping, and so they wait for a trip that rarely comes. This experiment says the opposite: the scenes worth photographing slide past your car window every day. Pin the spot, check the sun, and go back.
