The Editing Automation Stack

Three tools that save 2 hours per wedding without making your work look generic.

018
OPERATOR PROTOCOL

“3am. Third wedding in a row. My eyes were vibrating and I was still on the getting-ready shots — individually caressing sliders on 900 photos like each one was a gallery print. That's when I realized: I wasn't editing. I was performing editing, for an audience of no one, at an hourly rate approaching zero.”

The Myth

You've been told automation makes work look generic — that real editing is frame-by-frame craft. Frame-by-frame craft on 900 near-identical reception shots isn't craft. It's a refusal to systematize, and it costs you two hours a wedding.

The Truth

Eighty percent of wedding editing is repetition: same venue light, same skin tones, same corrections, hundreds of times. That's not artistry, that's manufacturing — and manufacturing is exactly what automation is for. The stack handles the repeatable 80%; your hands finish the 20% that actually carries your style. Generic comes from automating taste. This automates labor.

The Operator's Protocol — Build the Stack in 3 Steps
01
Forge one base preset. Take your best-edited frame from a typical venue and save every adjustment as a preset. This is your house look — built from your taste, not a download.
02
Batch and sync. Apply the base to the whole gallery, then sync in lighting batches — ceremony, reception, portraits. Correct once per batch, not once per frame.
03
Hand-finish the heroes. Pull the 30 to 50 frames that matter — the album candidates — and give them your full individual attention. This is where your style lives.
Pro tip: Rebuild the base preset once a year, not once a week. Constant tweaking reintroduces the exact inconsistency the stack exists to kill — let it settle and trust it.
The Payoff

Two hours back per wedding. Ten weddings a season is a work-week of your life, returned. And the galleries look more consistent, not less — because the base decisions stopped depending on how tired you were at 3am. You didn't need to suffer for your art. You needed a system.
