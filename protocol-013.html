How to Price Your First 10 Clients

A framework for setting rates that don't scare people away — and don't leave you broke.

013
OPERATOR PROTOCOL

“My first paid shoot: $75 for four hours, plus editing, plus driving across town. I was thrilled to be chosen. Then I did the math in the parking lot afterward and realized I'd earned roughly the same as the teenager handing out flyers next door — except she'd gone home at noon.”

The Myth

You've been told to start cheap, build the portfolio, and raise prices later. Later never comes. Cheap prices don't buy you experience — they buy you cheap clients, who refer more cheap clients, until cheap is your brand.

The Truth

Pricing isn't about worth. It's about math and nerve. The math: every hour shooting costs you roughly three hours total — shooting, editing, admin, delivery. So your price has to survive being divided by three. The nerve: say the number out loud without apologizing. The framework is simple — set one honest price for your first 10 clients, then raise it. Ten is enough to learn, few enough to outgrow.

The Operator's Protocol — Price It in 3 Steps
01
Do the real math. Estimate total hours — shoot plus edit plus admin. Multiply by the hourly rate you'd accept for hard, skilled work. That's your floor, not your price.
02
Set one number for ten. Pick the price and freeze it for your first 10 clients. No discounts, no “friends and family” erosion. Ten identical transactions teach you what the market actually thinks.
03
Raise at eleven. Client eleven pays more. That's the rule, decided now, before comfort talks you out of it. The first ten bought your education; eleven buys your business.
Pro tip: Never itemize your insecurity. “I'm new so I'm charging less” trains the client to value you less. The price is the price — the story behind it is yours alone.
The Payoff

You stop doing $75 funerals for your own ambition. Ten clients in, you have real revenue, real boundaries, and a price that survived contact with the market. You didn't need more portfolio pieces or more confidence. You needed arithmetic and a rule.
